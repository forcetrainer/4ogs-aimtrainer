# Motor learning and motor control science applied to FPS mouse aiming (notes compiled 2026-11-11)

Reading guide for the report writer:
- Every bullet under "Cited Findings" was retrieved in this research session; most were read from abstracts or search-result summaries, and only two papers (Listman 2021, Donovan 2022) were read in full text. Effect sizes should be checked against the primary PDF before being quoted as exact.
- Bullets under "Inferences" are my extrapolation. Bullets marked "background, not retrieved" are textbook-level knowledge I did not re-verify with a source in this session.
- Almost all FPS-specific empirical work comes from two commercially interested groups (Statespace/Aim Lab and NVIDIA Research). This is flagged where relevant.

## 1. Components of an aimed mouse movement (Fitts's law, speed-accuracy tradeoff, submovement models) and what is measurable from raw trajectories

### Takeaway
An aimed movement is well described as reaction time, then a fast pre-planned primary submovement, then zero or more feedback-based corrective submovements, then a click-timing decision; all of these are recoverable from timestamped raw mouse deltas plus target state. Skill must be estimated from the speed-accuracy tradeoff as a whole, because a single speed or accuracy number confounds skill with strategy.

### Cited Findings
- Meyer et al. (1988, Psychological Review 95:340-370) model rapid aimed movements as a primary submovement plus an optional corrective secondary submovement, tuned to minimise average movement time while keeping hits frequent; endpoint spread of the primary submovement grows with its velocity, and that velocity determines how often corrections are needed. — [search summary of Meyer 1988 and LJMU follow-up papers](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/15895/3/Optimization%20in%20manual%20aiming%20relating%20inherent%20variability%20and%20target%20size%2C%20and%20its%20influence%20on%20tendency.pdf)
- Elliott et al. (2010) multiple-process model keeps the ballistic-then-corrective structure but separates two online control processes: early "impulse regulation" (velocity and direction adjusted via feedforward comparison) and late "limb-target control". — [LJMU, Roberts et al.](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/2386/1/Common%20vs%20Independent.pdf)
- Meyer et al. proposed parsing criteria for the end of the first submovement (velocity zero-crossing, acceleration zero-crossing); an HCI paper argues these criteria often split 2D cursor movements into submovements that are not meaningful, so 2D parsing needs care. — [Springer, INTERACT 2009 chapter](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-642-03655-2_33.pdf)
- ISO 9241-9 throughput: ID = log2(A/We + 1), where We is the "effective width" computed from the observed endpoint distribution, and TP = ID/MT in bits/s. This folds speed and accuracy into one number. MacKenzie's group re-derived about 2.65 bps for the mouse from Card et al.'s data. — [MacKenzie, IJHCS 2004](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/ijhcs2004.html)
- Donovan et al. (2022, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience; N = 32 male pro and semi-pro players of Valorant, PUBG, Rainbow Six Siege; own hardware, remote; post hoc reanalysis) extracted per-shot kinematics by fitting a sigmoid to mouse position over time (fits with r2 < 0.5 dropped). Metrics: reaction time (target onset to 5% of movement endpoint), peak speed, accuracy (signed landing error as a percentage: hypometric vs hypermetric), precision (1 / median absolute percent error), "swipiness" (shot time relative to movement midpoint; about 0.5 means clicking mid-swipe, 1 or more means flick-then-land), and separately fitted corrective movements. — [Donovan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9744923)
- In the same study, "motor acuity" was defined from each player's speed-accuracy tradeoff curve across a large-target speed task (Gridshot) and a small-target precision task (Sixshot, targets about 14% of Gridshot size). Shorter reaction time and lower swipiness, and higher precision, correlated with higher motor acuity in both tasks (Spearman, p <= 8e-4); speed and accuracy also correlated in Gridshot. Coefficients are only in a figure, no R2 reported. — [Donovan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9744923)
- The same authors state that skill cannot be characterised from a single data point because skill and strategy are confounded, and that common esports metrics (kill-death ratio, damage, win rate) are unreliable measures of individual skill. Conflict of interest: all authors are officers or employees of Statespace Labs (Aim Lab). — [Donovan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9744923)
- Park and Lee's Intermittent Click Planning model (CHI 2020) treats the click as a timing decision made by an internal clock: users are intermittent controllers who form the click plan during the last submovement before the click. It predicted pointing error rates for stationary and moving targets with R2 = 0.992 and 0.985. — [Park & Lee, arXiv 1806.02973](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.02973)
- Lee and Oulasvirta (CHI 2016) model error rates in "temporal pointing" (pressing at the right moment): the user has an implicit point of aim but input timing is degraded by variability in three processes. — [Lee & Oulasvirta 2016](https://displaydaily.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/modeling-errors-chi2016.pdf)
- Do, Chang and Lee (CHI 2021) built a simulation model of point-and-click that combines intermittent motor control with click decision-making and applies to both stationary and moving targets. — [Do et al. 2021](https://dodoseung.github.io/paper/point_and_click_simulation_model.pdf)
- Yoon et al. (2025, Int. J. Human-Computer Studies vol. 199; NVIDIA Research co-authored) built a computational-rationality simulation of FPS aim-and-shoot that explicitly models the fact that mouse motion rotates the camera and therefore moves the target on screen; it reproduced behaviour of 20 FPS players (10 professionals) on key measures and attributed pro/amateur differences to distinct cognitive or motivational parameters. — [NVIDIA Research publication page](https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2025-04_modeling-visually-guided-aim-and-shoot-behavior-first-person-shoters)
- Boudaoud, Spjut and Kim (IEEE Conference on Games 2022, extended 2023; NVIDIA): a generally optimal first-person sensitivity range of about 0.45 to 1.8 degrees/mm (a roughly 4x range, consistent with CD-gain literature). Too low slows view rotation; too high reduces precision and adds corrective movements; both lengthen completion time. Low-ID (easy, large/near) targets favour higher sensitivity and high-ID targets favour lower. They did not observe a long-term "hard-wired" muscle memory of a specific sensitivity for all players. — [Boudaoud et al., arXiv 2203.12050](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.12050)

### Inferences
- Measurable from raw mouse deltas plus target geometry, with no extra hardware: reaction time (onset to movement threshold), primary-submovement amplitude and signed endpoint error (undershoot vs overshoot), peak velocity and time to peak, number and duration of corrective submovements, time from last submovement end to click ("settle/confirmation time"), click-relative-to-movement phase (Donovan's swipiness), endpoint scatter (effective width) and ISO-style throughput, and for tracking tasks time-on-target and lag/error time series.
- Not measurable from the mouse alone: gaze behaviour, grip, joint usage (wrist vs arm), and perceptual/decision latency separate from motor latency (only their sum is visible as reaction time).
- A per-user speed-accuracy operating curve (at least two target sizes or two instructed emphases) is a more defensible skill metric than a leaderboard score, and it gives a diagnostic: is the user slow-and-accurate, fast-and-sloppy, or losing time in corrections or in click confirmation.
- Submovement parsing in 2D/3D view-angle space is noisy; a robust implementation should smooth velocity, use speed (not per-axis velocity) minima, and treat the parse as approximate.
- Background, not retrieved this session: Woodworth (1899) originated the two-component (initial impulse plus current control) idea; Fitts (1954) and the Schmidt et al. (1979) linear speed-accuracy law for very brief movements; visual feedback corrections need roughly 100-200 ms, so very short flicks are effectively open loop.

### Gaps
- Donovan et al. note Fitts's law did not hold cleanly in their Aim Lab tasks and attribute it (speculatively) to strategy differences; I did not find an independent test of Fitts's law for 3D first-person view rotation by a non-vendor lab.
- No retrieved study gives normative values (for example typical number of corrective submovements or click-confirmation time) for casual or intermediate players. Donovan's sample is pros only.
- The correlation coefficients in Donovan et al. were not extractable from text.

## 2. Practice structure: what the motor learning literature says, with effect sizes and replication status

### Takeaway
The classic "principles" are less solid than textbooks imply. Spacing practice and judging learning by delayed retention/transfer rather than in-session score are the best supported; contextual interference (interleaving) has a medium effect in lab tasks that shrinks to small or non-significant in applied settings; external focus, self-controlled practice and reduced feedback frequency have all had their effects shrink toward zero under bias-corrected meta-analysis.

### Cited Findings
Learning vs performance
- Soderstrom and Bjork (2015, Perspectives on Psychological Science 10:176-199) review motor and verbal learning and conclude that performance during acquisition is an unreliable index of learning (durable change shown by delayed retention or transfer); manipulations can have opposite effects on performance and learning, and learners commonly misjudge acquisition performance as learning. — [PubMed 25910388](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25910388/)

Deliberate practice
- Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald (2014, Psychological Science; 88 studies, more than 11,000 participants): deliberate practice explained 26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education, under 1% in professions; about 12% overall. Conclusion: important, but less than claimed. — [Macnamara et al. 2014 PDF](https://www.psychologie.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:658d39fe-2f4b-467f-9ae2-ffeb25caf2b6/McNamara.PsychSci.25.2014.pdf); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/07/140716123835.htm)
- Macnamara, Moreau and Hambrick (2016): deliberate practice accounted for 18% of variance in sports performance overall but only 1% among elite-level athletes. Ericsson disputed the definitions used, and the authors replied. — [Macnamara et al. 2016 PDF](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/macnamara-et-al-2016-the-relationship-between-deliberate-practice-and-performance-in-sports-a-meta-analysis.pdf); [Reply to Ericsson](https://scottbarrykaufman.com///wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Macnamara-et-al.-2016-Reply-to-Ericsson.pdf)

Contextual interference (blocked vs random/interleaved)
- Brady (2004) meta-analysis, 61 studies: overall effect about 0.38; basic/lab research 0.57 vs applied research 0.19. — [Brady 2004 PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2004-brady.pdf)
- Czyż, Wójcik and Solarská (2024, Frontiers in Psychology), transfer: 42 studies in review, 34 in meta-analysis. Pooled effect on delayed transfer SMD = 0.55 favouring random practice; laboratory studies SMD = 0.75 (significant); applied studies SMD = 0.34 (not significant); adults 0.54, older adults 1.28, young participants 0.12 (negligible). — [Czyż et al. 2024, PMC11349744](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11349744/)
- Companion retention meta-analysis (Czyż et al., Scientific Reports; listed as 2023 in one index and July 2024 in another): medium benefit of high contextual interference overall, effective in laboratory settings but almost negligible in applied settings; large in older adults, medium in adults, negligible in young participants. The transfer paper acknowledges the overall CI effect "has faced skepticism". — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/bf930964668d4780ba4c1beda434288d); [Czyż et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11349744/)

Variability of practice / schema theory
- Schmidt's schema theory predicts that variable practice within a movement class builds a schema that improves retention and transfer; Lee and colleagues argued the benefit of variable practice may come from contextual interference rather than schema formation. — [Czyż et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11349744/)
- A Frontiers commentary is titled around how little is firmly known about variability of practice and information processing ("How Much Do We Know?"). — [Czyż 2021 commentary, PMC8371322](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8371322/)

Challenge point framework
- Guadagnoli and Lee (2004, Journal of Motor Behavior 36:212-224) propose that learning depends on information available, which depends on functional task difficulty relative to the learner's skill; there is an optimal challenge point, and the paper frames contextual interference and feedback effects as interactions of skill level and task difficulty. It is a theoretical framework that generates testable hypotheses. — [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Challenge_point_framework)
- A 2025 systematic scoping review (Thomas et al., Journal of Motor Behavior 57:444-462) maps applications of the framework rather than testing whether its predictions hold; a 2021 Journal of Sports Sciences paper describes the original framework as mostly theoretical. Evidence from individual experiments is mixed (for example Sanli and Lee found overly challenging practice hindered learning). — [Scoping review, Leeds Beckett repository](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/12281/1/ChallengeAcceptedASystematicScopingReviewOfTheapplicationsOfTheChallengePointFrameworkPV-JONES.pdf); [Hodges & Lohse extended challenge framework preprint](https://msl.kin.educ.ubc.ca/sites/default/files/publication-files/PrePrint_ChallengeFrameworkforCOACHING_dEC2021_JSS.pdf)

Spacing / distributed practice
- Donovan and Radosevich (1999) meta-analysis, 63 studies, 112 effect sizes: mean weighted effect 0.46 favouring spaced over massed practice. A later review reports their motor-task subset (40 effect sizes) averaged d = 0.76, and Lee and Genovese (1988) found d = 0.79 across 73 motor effect sizes. Moderator: task type matters, and for some skill tasks longer inter-trial lags reduced the benefit. — [Donovan & Radosevich 1999 PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/1999-donovan.pdf); [Cepeda et al. 2006](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf)

Sleep consolidation
- Schmid et al. (2020, Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews): 48 studies, 53 sleep vs 53 wake groups, small overall relative sleep gain in motor memory consolidation, g = 0.43 (mostly finger-tapping and mirror-tracing tasks). — [Schmid et al. 2020](https://boris.unibe.ch/146534)
- Pan and Rickard (2015, Psychological Bulletin; 34 articles on explicit motor sequence learning) contest this: post-sleep gains are largely an artefact of averaging over online learning and of massed-practice (fatigue/reactive inhibition) confounds; they conclude sleep does not enhance motor learning and that a stabilising role cannot be determined from the literature. — [Pan & Rickard 2015](https://zendy.io/pdf-viewer/10.1037/bul0000009)
- King et al. (2017) review: sleep-related motor consolidation depends on boundary conditions including task features, and the literature is equivocal. — [PubMed 28465166](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28465166/)

Feedback frequency / guidance hypothesis
- Meta-analysis by McKay and colleagues of reduced relative feedback frequency: substantial heterogeneity, no significant moderators, high uncertainty, and no significant effect of reduced feedback frequency at any time point (reported g = .21 at delayed retention vs .06 at immediate retention, difference not significant, p = .47). Conclusion: robust evidence on feedback frequency and motor learning is lacking. — [preprint record, osf.io/v2cp7](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.31234/osf.io/v2cp7); [AMiner record](https://www.aminer.org/pub/623008115aee126c0fa6459b/meta-analysis-of-the-reduced-relative-feedback-frequency-effect-on-motor-learning)
- A 2024 primary study notes the guidance hypothesis remains controversial, with supporting and contradicting results. — [PMC10933749](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10933749/)

External vs internal focus of attention
- Uncorrected meta-analysis (Chua et al. 2021): external focus advantage g = 0.264 for performance (73 studies, 1,824 participants) and g = 0.583 for retention (40 studies, 1,274 participants). — [UNLV record of Chua et al.](https://digitalscholarship.unlv.edu/kns_fac_articles/428)
- McKay et al. (2024, Psychological Bulletin 150:1347-1362, "Reporting Bias, Not External Focus") reanalysed seven prior meta-analyses with robust Bayesian methods: moderate-to-strong evidence of publication bias in every analysis; bias-corrected effects g = 0.01 (performance), 0.15 (retention), 0.09 (transfer); Bayes factors favoured the null; clear heterogeneity, suggesting unknown moderators. — [McKay et al. 2024 PDF](https://raw.github.com/cartermaclab/carterlab-papers/main/2024_mckay-etal_reporting-bias-not-external-focus-a-robust-bayesian-meta-analysis-and-systematic-review-of-the-external-focus-of-attention.pdf)

Self-controlled practice / enhanced expectancies (OPTIMAL theory components)
- McKay, Yantha, Hussien, Carter and Ste-Marie (Meta-Psychology 2022): naive estimate g = .44 (k = 52); selection-model corrected g = .107 (95% CI .047 to .18); the literature is described as underpowered, biased and lacking evidential value. A 2023 robust Bayesian follow-up reached the same conclusion for self-control and enhanced expectancies: true effects small, uncertain, possibly null. — [Meta-Psychology article](https://conferences.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2803); [McKay et al. 2023 PDF](https://raw.github.com/cartermaclab/carterlab-papers/main/2023_mckay-etal_the-combination-of-reporting-bias-and-underpowered-study-designs-has-substantially-exaggerated-the-motor-learning-benefits-of.pdf)

Specificity of practice
- Proteau's specificity hypothesis: learning is specific to the sensory (afferent) information available during practice; supported in a tracking task by Coull, Tremblay and Elliott (2001), though specificity did not grow with amount of practice. — [Coull et al. 2001 reference page](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/02701367.2001.10608971)
- A scoping review of motor transfer studies found positive transfer in 36% of studies, no change in 25%, mixed results in 25%, and negative transfer in 14%. — [JEMS scoping review](https://www.scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/2548)

### Inferences
- Confidence ranking for design purposes: (strong) spacing beats massing; in-session score is not learning; transfer is narrow and far from guaranteed. (moderate, lab-dependent) interleaving and variation help retention/transfer in adults but the applied effect is small. (weak or contested) reduced feedback frequency, external-focus cueing, learner choice as a learning enhancer, sleep-specific gains, and any precise "optimal challenge" percentage.
- Mouse aiming in a trainer resembles the simple laboratory tasks where contextual interference effects are largest (short discrete movements, adult participants), so interleaving target sizes, distances and task types is a reasonable bet, but the benefit in the "applied" sense (in-game aim) is untested.
- The Macnamara figure for games (26%) is from chess-like games and is correlational; it says accumulated structured practice matters substantially but most variance lies elsewhere. It should temper marketing claims, and it does not tell a casual player that practice is futile.
- Since bias-corrected effects for learner autonomy are near zero for learning, offering choice is justified on motivation/adherence grounds, not as a learning accelerator.
- Background, not retrieved: the classic guidance-hypothesis studies (Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter 1984; Winstein & Schmidt 1990), Shea & Morgan (1979) for contextual interference, Wulf & Lewthwaite (2016) OPTIMAL theory.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis isolating variability of practice (schema theory) with a pooled effect size; only the CI meta-analyses and a commentary.
- No quantitative test of the challenge point framework's "optimal" difficulty level (for example a target success rate) was found. Any specific hit-rate target (such as 70-85%) would be extrapolation, not evidence from this search.
- Knowledge of results vs knowledge of performance: I found no meta-analytic comparison relevant to pointing tasks.
- Exact published (not preprint) corrected estimates for the feedback-frequency and self-control meta-analyses were not confirmed from full text.

## 3. Learning curves, plateaus, session length and diminishing returns

### Takeaway
Improvement is steep early and slows (individual curves are closer to exponential than power-law), with plateaus that typically end when the performer changes method, often after a temporary dip. The only large aim-specific dataset suggests most of a day's benefit comes within roughly 30 to 50 minutes of focused practice, with about an hour as the point of maximum benefit, and that short breaks of a day or two cost little.

### Cited Findings
- The power law of practice dates to Snoddy (1928) and Newell and Rosenbloom (1981). Heathcote, Brown and Mewhort (2000) fitted 40 data sets and found the exponential function fit better than the power function in all unaveraged (individual) data sets; averaging across learners biases toward a power function. Later work adds that learning rates differ across individuals. — [Wikipedia: Power law of practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_law_of_practice); [Heathcote et al. 2000 PDF](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-power-law-repealed-the-case-for-an-exponential-law-of-3ur3vq1jx1.pdf)
- Gray and Lindstedt (2017, Cognitive Science 41:1838-1870): plateaus, dips and leaps mark where performers invent or adopt new methods; dips are exploration periods where performance drops while a new method is tried, leaps are when it succeeds and performance rises above the smooth log-log curve. They use changepoint analysis on individual data. — [PhilPapers record](https://api.philpapers.org/rec/GRAPDA)
- Listman et al. (2021, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience): observational Aim Lab data, N = 7,174 players, 62,170 game days, 682,564 sixty-second Gridshot runs; estimated about 90% male, median age 18. Self-selected schedules, one task only, proprietary data. Authors are employees of or consultants to Statespace Labs, which funded the study. — [Listman et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2021.777779/full)
- In that data, median hits per second rose from 2.72 on day 1 to 3.78 on day 60 with no asymptote reached (ten top players had a median of 6.25), while hit rate (accuracy) rose only from 86.1% to 89.6%. Most gain was in speed at near-constant accuracy. Better baseline predicted smaller gains. — [Listman et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2021.777779/full)
- There is a daily warm-up effect: hits per second improved quickly over the first few runs each day (the authors hypothesise re-adaptation to mouse sensitivity). Only about 65% of a day's within-session gain was retained to the next day early on, falling to about 40% after day 10 (abstract says 40 to 60%). — [Listman et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2021.777779/full)
- Diminishing returns: benefit to next-day performance was non-monotonic in number of daily runs, peaking near 75 one-minute runs; 90% of the benefit was reached at about 49 runs (about 50 minutes) per the Results, while the abstract states 90% of the benefit at 30 minutes per day. This is an internal inconsistency in the paper. Maximum benefit was about one hour with warm-up. — [Listman et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2021.777779/full)
- Skipped days had a small negative effect on hits per second that was not statistically significant (p = 0.13). Practice-mode runs were significantly worse than compete-mode runs, which the authors read as lower effort. — [Listman et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2021.777779/full)
- Huang et al. (2017, Topics in Cognitive Science; Halo: Reach and StarCraft 2 match data, observational): players with the most matches per week gained the most total skill but not the most per match; the most efficient gains per match were at about four to eight matches per week. Breaks of one or two days were not harmful; after a 30-day break about 10 matches were needed to recover. Elite StarCraft players used consistent warm-up routines. — [Brown University news release](https://www.brown.edu/news/2017-02-27/Gameskill)
- Rogers et al. (2024, Frontiers in Sports and Active Living; N = 10 FPS esports players, two sessions three to five days apart): KovaaK's metrics for micro flicking, macro flicking, strafe tracking and wall peeking showed good-to-excellent test-retest reliability; the only significant between-session changes were in macro flicking accuracy (p = .005) and hits per second (p = .009). This is a reliability study, not a training study; a vendor blog misreports it as evidence that training works. — [Rogers et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1309991/pdf)

### Inferences
- A defensible default dose for casual players is 10 to 30 minutes per day, most days: it sits on the steep part of the Listman dose-response curve, well under the roughly one-hour point of maximum benefit, and frequency with short gaps is supported by both Listman and Huang. The exact number is extrapolation from one vendor-affiliated observational dataset on one task.
- Because only 40 to 65% of within-session gain carries over to the next day, the first run(s) of each day (before warm-up) are the honest measure of learning; last-run or best-run scores overstate it. This aligns with the Soderstrom and Bjork distinction.
- A warm-up block should be excluded from progress metrics, and a brief warm-up before real matches is plausibly useful as performance preparation (distinct from learning).
- Plateaus in a trainer may reflect a stable method (for example always confirming before clicking, or a fixed sensitivity/grip) rather than a ceiling. Breaking them likely requires changing the constraint (smaller targets, time pressure, speed-emphasis vs accuracy-emphasis blocks) and tolerating a temporary score dip; the product should warn users that a dip after a technique change is expected. This applies Gray and Lindstedt; it is not tested for aiming.
- Listman's gains are on the trained task (Gridshot) itself; they say nothing about transfer to games.

### Gaps
- No randomised controlled trial was found testing whether aim-trainer practice transfers to in-game performance (searched for Aim Lab/KovaaK's transfer studies; found only the reliability pilot and vendor blogs). This is the largest evidence gap for the product.
- No controlled study of session length for mouse aiming; Listman's dose-response is observational and self-selected (motivated players may both practise more and learn faster).
- No data found on fatigue-related decline within long aiming sessions or on optimal rest intervals between runs.

## 4. Tracking (pursuit) vs discrete pointing; gaze, smooth pursuit and eye-hand coordination in FPS players

### Takeaway
Theory and the HCI models treat tracking and click-timing on moving targets as distinct control problems from discrete flicks (continuous or intermittent feedback control vs pre-planned impulse plus correction), but I found no direct factor-analytic evidence that they are separable abilities in gamers. Gaze evidence in FPS players is recent, small-sample and lab-based: experienced players tend to reach the target with a single saccade that the crosshair then follows, and "quiet eye" findings do not transfer cleanly to FPS.

### Cited Findings
- Yang et al. (2025, Computers in Human Behavior): 28 experienced FPS players (2 or more years, weekly play) vs 35 non-players on a simplified aiming task adapted from a Counter-Strike training map. Experienced players were faster and most often showed a "0-fixation-1-saccade" pattern (one saccade straight from target appearance to the hit), while non-players needed more corrective eye movements. The final saccade before the shot matched the crosshair's path, interpreted as the motor-planning signal. Limitations: no realistic game scenarios, no mouse movement data recorded. — [PsyPost summary of Yang et al.](https://www.psypost.org/experienced-fps-gamers-show-faster-more-efficient-eye-movements-during-aiming-tasks-study-finds/)
- Sciarra and Coco (2025) reported that the duration of target fixation before the hand response does not linearly predict performance in these games; a pilot study by Hampshire and Tyndall (2024) found the quiet-eye duration effect in FPS was reduced with multiple targets. Both were seen only as search-result summaries. — [Sapienza IRIS record](https://iris.uniroma1.it/handle/11573/1742105); [University of Chichester eprint](https://eprints.chi.ac.uk/id/eprint/7732/)
- A 2024 Research Square preprint (not peer reviewed) reports FPS players outperforming MOBA players on visual reaction time and hand-eye coordination tests. — [Research Square preprint](https://assets-eu.researchsquare.com/files/rs-4528756/v1_covered_6747e2bf-03e1-466f-b2fa-0a3167b3aaf5.pdf)
- Vendor/secondary material (SR Research case study, a Woojer blog describing gaze-centering feedback training in 21 players) could not be traced to a verifiable primary study; treat as marketing. — [SR Research case study](https://www.sr-research.com/case-studies/eye-tracking-expert-esports-players/)
- Pursuit tracking: Parker et al. (2017, Attention, Perception & Psychophysics; 20 adults, 1-minute pursuit-tracking trials) fitted perceptual-control models to individuals and found person-specific, stable tracking parameters; earlier control-theory work found individual differences in visual-motor tracking persisted across practice. — [Parker et al. 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-017-1398-2)
- Moving-target clicking has its own models: the Intermittent Click Planning model covers stationary and moving targets and treats click timing as a separate decision from pointing (R2 = 0.985 for moving targets); the Do et al. simulation likewise handles moving targets through intermittent control. — [Park & Lee](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.02973); [Do et al. 2021](https://dodoseung.github.io/paper/point_and_click_simulation_model.pdf)
- Coull, Tremblay and Elliott (2001) found specificity of practice in a tracking task (learning tied to the feedback conditions practised). — [reference page](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/02701367.2001.10608971)
- KovaaK's reliability pilot treats flicking (micro, macro), strafe tracking and wall peeking as separate task families with separately reliable metrics. — [Rogers et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1309991/pdf)

### Inferences
- Given specificity of practice and narrow transfer in the motor literature, the safe design assumption is that flicking, tracking, target switching and click-timing on moving targets should each be trained and measured separately; do not expect flick practice to improve tracking much. This is inference from general principles, not a measured dissociation.
- Tracking metrics should differ from flick metrics: time-on-target percentage, mean angular error, response lag to target direction changes (reaction to reversal), and smoothness, rather than time-to-hit.
- The Yang et al. gaze finding suggests the eye lands first and the crosshair follows the saccade vector; a trainer cannot measure this without an eye tracker, but an instruction to look at the target (not the crosshair) is consistent with it. Whether instructing gaze improves learning is untested, and bias-corrected attentional-focus effects are near zero.
- First-person aiming differs from 2D pointing in that the hand moves the camera and so the whole visual scene (Yoon et al.), which probably limits smooth-pursuit use during large flicks. Speculative.

### Gaps
- No study found correlating tracking and flicking ability across individuals (for example factor analysis of trainer scenario scores). The community's taxonomy (clicking / tracking / switching) is practitioner lore, not validated psychometrics, as far as this search found.
- No peer-reviewed data on smooth-pursuit eye movement quality in FPS players during tracking scenarios was found.
- No evidence found on whether gaze training improves aiming in FPS.

## 5. FPS-specific motor research: models, kinematics, grip/arm vs wrist, sensitivity, ergonomics

### Takeaway
The FPS-specific literature is small (roughly a dozen papers), dominated by vendor-affiliated groups and by expert or self-selected samples. It supports kinematic decomposition of flicks, a broad optimal sensitivity band rather than a single magic value, and expertise-related differences in wrist use, but it does not establish that any grip, aiming style or sensitivity causes better aim.

### Cited Findings
- Kinematic profile of expert flicks (Donovan et al. 2022, N = 32 pros): faster reaction time, less "swipiness" (land then click rather than click mid-swipe), higher precision and higher peak speed go with higher motor acuity. — [Donovan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9744923)
- Long-term practice in the wild (Listman et al. 2021, N = 7,174): continued improvement over 60 to 100 days without ceiling; details in section 3. — [Listman et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2021.777779/full)
- Wrist vs arm: a CHI 2024 paper introduced a dual-sensor mouse method to quantify reliance on wrist movement (W-index). Expertise and mouse sensitivity significantly affected wrist-aiming habits; degree of wrist aiming correlated positively with upper-limb workload; professional FPS players averaged a W-index 20.2% higher than amateurs. Main FPS study N = 17. It did not test which style is more accurate. — [CHI 2024, doi 10.1145/3613904.3642797](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642797)
- Sensitivity: optimal band of about 0.45 to 1.8 degrees/mm; easy targets favour higher and hard targets lower sensitivity; no universal long-term "muscle memory" for one sensitivity; first-person sensitivity is not directly convertible to pointer CD gain. — [Boudaoud et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.12050)
- Aim-and-shoot simulation (Yoon et al. 2025; 20 players, 10 pros) and the Lee-lab click models (Park & Lee 2020; Do et al. 2021; Lee & Oulasvirta 2016) provide mechanistic accounts of click timing, intermittent control and camera-coupled target motion. — [Yoon et al.](https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2025-04_modeling-visually-guided-aim-and-shoot-behavior-first-person-shoters); [Park & Lee](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.02973); [Do et al.](https://dodoseung.github.io/paper/point_and_click_simulation_model.pdf); [Lee & Oulasvirta](https://displaydaily.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/modeling-errors-chi2016.pdf)
- Measurement reliability: KovaaK's task metrics are test-retest reliable in 10 esports players. — [Rogers et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1309991/pdf)
- Community lore and vendor marketing: claims about arm vs wrist superiority, mouse weight, polling rate and contact points come from forums and peripheral-maker blogs, several of which state they are theoretical estimates and not controlled studies. — [example vendor article](https://attackshark.com/blogs/knowledges/arm-aiming-8k-polling-high-sweep-motion-analysis); [example forum thread](https://us.forums.blizzard.com/en/overwatch/t/arm-aim-vs-wrist-aim/570356)

### Inferences
- The "never change your sensitivity or you lose muscle memory" belief is not supported by Boudaoud et al.; a trainer can let users vary or test sensitivity within the broad optimal band, and Listman's daily warm-up effect suggests re-adaptation is fast (minutes). Whether deliberate sensitivity variation aids learning is unproven community practice.
- The pro vs amateur wrist difference is correlational and could reflect sensitivity choice, game genre or selection; combined with the workload correlation, the product should not prescribe a grip or joint strategy.
- The Lee-lab models imply that a "missed" shot can be a pointing error or a click-timing error; a trainer could separate these by checking whether the crosshair was on target shortly before or after the click.

### Gaps
- No controlled ergonomics or posture study for FPS mouse aiming was found (injury risk, desk height, arm support). Only the workload correlation from the CHI 2024 paper.
- No independent (non-vendor) replication of the Aim Lab kinematic or dose-response findings was found.
- No peer-reviewed study of casual or intermediate players' aiming kinematics over a training intervention was found.
- I did not retrieve other Lee-lab esports papers that may exist (for example on latency or moving-target input); coverage of that lab is partial.

## 6. Evidence-supported design rules for a trainer (strong evidence vs extrapolation)

### Takeaway
Only a few rules rest on strong evidence: keep sessions short and spread across days, measure learning with delayed (start-of-next-session) retention and speed-accuracy curves rather than in-session high scores, and expect narrow transfer so train the task variants the target game needs. Interleaving and variation are a reasonable moderate-evidence default; difficulty targeting, feedback thinning and attentional cues are plausible but currently weakly supported.

### Cited Findings
Stronger support
- Distributed practice outperforms massed practice for motor tasks (d about 0.46 overall, about 0.76 for motor subsets). — [Donovan & Radosevich 1999](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/1999-donovan.pdf); [Cepeda et al. 2006](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf)
- In-session performance is not a reliable index of learning; use delayed retention and transfer tests. — [Soderstrom & Bjork 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25910388/)
- Aim practice shows diminishing returns within a day (90% of benefit at about 30 to 50 minutes, maximum near one hour), a daily warm-up effect, partial overnight retention (40 to 65%), and little cost to short breaks. Observational, vendor-affiliated. — [Listman et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2021.777779/full)
- Skill should be measured from the speed-accuracy tradeoff across at least two difficulty levels, since single-point metrics confound skill and strategy. — [Donovan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9744923)
- Transfer between motor tasks is unreliable: positive in only 36% of studies in a scoping review. — [JEMS scoping review](https://www.scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/2548)

Moderate support
- Random/interleaved practice improves delayed retention and transfer with a medium effect in adults in lab tasks (SMD about 0.55 to 0.75), small and non-significant in applied settings (0.34; Brady: 0.19). — [Czyż et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11349744/); [Brady 2004](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2004-brady.pdf)
- Sensitivity within about 0.45 to 1.8 degrees/mm is broadly fine; harder targets favour lower values. — [Boudaoud et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.12050)

Weak or contested support
- External focus (bias-corrected g about 0.01 to 0.15), self-controlled practice (corrected g about 0.11), reduced feedback frequency (no significant effect), sleep-specific gains (g = 0.43 but disputed), and challenge point (theoretical, no validated optimum). — [McKay et al. 2024](https://raw.github.com/cartermaclab/carterlab-papers/main/2024_mckay-etal_reporting-bias-not-external-focus-a-robust-bayesian-meta-analysis-and-systematic-review-of-the-external-focus-of-attention.pdf); [McKay et al. 2022](https://conferences.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2803); [feedback frequency meta-analysis](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.31234/osf.io/v2cp7); [Schmid et al. 2020](https://boris.unibe.ch/146534); [Pan & Rickard 2015](https://zendy.io/pdf-viewer/10.1037/bul0000009); [challenge point scoping review](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/12281/1/ChallengeAcceptedASystematicScopingReviewOfTheapplicationsOfTheChallengePointFrameworkPV-JONES.pdf)

### Inferences
Proposed rules, each labelled by evidence grade (S = strong general motor-learning evidence, M = moderate/lab-dependent, E = extrapolation):
1. (S) Default to short daily sessions, roughly 10 to 30 minutes, hard-capped well under an hour; encourage 4 to 6 days per week over long single sessions. The specific minute figures are E, drawn from one observational dataset.
2. (S) Report progress from a fixed, short "cold" benchmark at the start of a session (before warm-up) and from periodic delayed retention checks, not from best-ever or end-of-session scores. De-emphasise leaderboards and per-scenario high scores, which reward task-specific strategy.
3. (S for the measurement principle, E for implementation) Score skill as a speed-accuracy profile: run each drill at two or more target sizes or with speed-emphasis and accuracy-emphasis blocks, and show throughput/effective-width style metrics plus a decomposition (reaction time, primary flick error and direction of bias, corrections, click confirmation time).
4. (M) Interleave task types and vary target size, distance, direction and (for tracking) speed and motion pattern within a session rather than repeating one scenario in a block. Warn users that interleaved practice feels worse and scores lower in-session; this is the expected performance-learning dissociation.
5. (S for narrow transfer, E for the remedy) Train the variants the user's game actually needs: separate modules for flicking, tracking, target switching and moving-target click timing, with target sizes, distances, movement speeds, field of view and sensitivity matched to the target game. Do not promise in-game improvement; no RCT shows trainer-to-game transfer.
6. (E, theory-guided) Adapt difficulty so the user is neither at ceiling nor failing most trials, using per-user staircases on target size/speed. Present any numeric success-rate target as a design choice, not an evidence-based constant.
7. (E) On a plateau, change the constraint (speed-emphasis block, smaller targets, a different motion pattern) and tell the user a temporary dip is expected (Gray and Lindstedt).
8. (weak) Feedback: there is no good evidence that thinning feedback helps, and none that it hurts. Prefer a few diagnostic kinematic summaries after a block (knowledge of performance derived from the trajectory) over constant in-trial score popups, on usability grounds rather than on the guidance hypothesis.
9. (weak) Attentional cues ("look at the target, not the crosshair") and learner choice are cheap and may help motivation, but should not be sold as evidence-based learning accelerators.
10. (E) Exclude the first few warm-up runs from learning metrics; offer a 2 to 3 minute warm-up routine before playing the real game as a performance (not learning) aid.
11. (E) Do not prescribe grip, wrist vs arm style or a single sensitivity; show the broad optimal sensitivity band and let users test within it.
12. (weak) Sleep: advising practice on separate days is supported by spacing evidence regardless of whether sleep itself adds a consolidation benefit.

### Gaps
- The central product claim (trainer practice improves in-game aim for casual or intermediate players) has no direct experimental support that I could find as of November 2026; an in-product transfer measurement (for example opt-in comparison with in-game accuracy stats) would be novel evidence.
- No evidence was found on the optimal ratio of trainer practice to actual game play.
- Nothing retrieved addresses the casual-to-intermediate population specifically; findings come from lab novices on simple tasks, self-selected Aim Lab users (median age 18, about 90% male) or professionals.
- Search budget did not allow full-text verification of most meta-analyses; numbers above marked as coming from abstracts/summaries should be re-checked before publication.
