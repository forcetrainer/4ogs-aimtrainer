# Build a trainer that measures transfer

No controlled study shows that practice in a standalone aim trainer improves in-game results, so the honest design goal is a trainer that tests its own usefulness on you and your friends. The published research establishes three narrower things: people get better at trainer tasks with practice, trainer scores are reliable measurements, and skilled FPS players score better on aiming tasks than unskilled ones. General motor-learning and perceptual-learning science adds a few solid rules (spread practice across days, judge learning by delayed tests, expect transfer to be narrow and tied to similarity) and a longer list of popular rules that shrink toward zero under scrutiny. Your six concerns mostly hold up: abstract sphere visuals keep the motor task intact but remove the perceptual one; fixed-scenario benchmarks are structurally a "teach to the test" setup; benchmark rank has no published link to game rank; commercial "AI coaching" has no outcome evidence; sensitivity is a broad plateau where the right output is a range; and target detection is trainable in the lab with gains lasting months, but only for backgrounds similar to those trained. The design that follows is a small Windows trainer whose value is correctness and data: game-matched input and FOV, parameter-randomised drills that include a detect-identify-acquire recognition mode, self-referenced progress with noise bands in place of ranks, and an LLM confined to wording. Most FPS-specific findings come from two commercially interested groups (Statespace/Aim Lab and NVIDIA), many sources were read only as abstracts, and every specific design number below is a starting point to tune, which is why the build path starts with instrumentation.

## Nobody has shown trainer practice improves in-game play

### What the trainer studies measured

Every located study using Aim Lab or KovaaK's data measures performance inside the trainer. The flagship paper analysed **7,174 Aim Lab players and 682,564 sixty-second Gridshot runs**, and its outcomes were hit rate and hits per second; the authors state they had no information on what other games players played ([Listman et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8720934)). It is observational, single-task, uses proprietary data, and was funded by Statespace, the maker of Aim Lab, whose employees and a paid consultant wrote it. A follow-up on 32 professional and semi-professional players is kinematic, reports only p-values, and explicitly leaves the correlation of Aim Lab scores with in-game results as future work; all authors are Statespace officers or employees ([Donovan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9744923)). The one independent KovaaK's paper is a test-retest reliability pilot with ten players, and its authors caution against extrapolating to in-game performance because the trainer isolates aiming while real play adds cognitive load ([Rogers et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10925653/)).

The closest things to transfer evidence are weak. An IEEE conference paper on a one-week Aim Lab routine and Valorant damage per round could not be retrieved, and secondary summaries disagree on whether it had 6 or 30 participants ([IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9436822/)). A randomised study of 21 novices found that roughly 30 hours of CS:GO deathmatch against bots improved in-engine aim tests in both groups, which shows that structured in-game practice already works and sets the bar a trainer must beat for the same time spent ([Neri et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8224404/)). Marketing statistics that circulate, including a "27% KDA improvement" figure, could not be traced to any study ([secondary review](https://humanbenchmark.now/aim-trainer-faq/do-aim-trainers-transfer-to-real-games)).

The cross-sectional pattern is real. Self-reported FPS players acquired targets faster than non-players (588 ms against 727 ms) in an independent browser study, although gaming hours did not predict performance ([Warburton et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10411933/)). That pattern fits transfer, and it fits reverse causation (playing the game builds aim) and self-selection equally well.

### What transfer science predicts

The broader literature predicts gains that are largest on the trained task and shrink with dissimilarity. A review of the studies cited by brain-training companies found improvement on trained tasks and no compelling evidence of real-world benefit ([Simons et al. 2016, APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/brain-training.html)). Far transfer "rarely occurs" across chess, music and working-memory training ([Sala & Gobet 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721417712760)). A scoping review of motor transfer studies found positive transfer in only **36% of studies** ([JEMS scoping review](https://www.scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/2548)). Motor skill can be startlingly specific: skilled basketball players shoot better from the free-throw line than their performance at neighbouring distances predicts ([Keetch et al. 2005](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2005-keetch.pdf)).

Two findings pull the other way and keep the picture from being bleak. First-person camera aiming and ordinary cursor pointing have nearly identical spatial kinematics, so raw mouse control looks like a fairly general capacity ([Warburton et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10411933/)). And practice does move trainer performance for months without a ceiling. The reasonable reading is that some basic pointing precision transfers, and that score gains on a repeated fixed scenario substantially overstate it. That reading is inference; no aim-trainer study manipulates task fidelity and measures transfer.

### Why benchmark ranks become their own game

Your second and third concerns have indirect empirical support. In the Aim Lab data, median hits per second rose from **2.72 on day 1 to 3.78 on day 60** while hit rate moved only from 86.1% to 89.6% and plateaued within about five days, so gains were mostly speed at near-constant accuracy ([Listman et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8720934)). Gridshot elicits a distinct "swipier" strategy of clicking mid-movement, and a single Fitts's-law model failed across two tasks that differed mainly in target size, which means task-specific strategies form quickly ([Donovan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9744923)). Experienced players improved on macro flicking across just two sessions of a reliability test ([Rogers et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10925653/)). A fixed scenario set replayed for high scores rewards exactly this kind of scenario-specific learning, and best-of-many scoring inflates through variance. That conclusion follows from the specificity literature and these observations; nobody has quantified it.

No published correlation between Voltaic, KovaaK's or Aim Lab rank and competitive rank was found. Voltaic itself says its benchmarks "measure your raw aiming abilities" and are one piece of the puzzle ([Voltaic Season 5 announcement](https://blog.voltaic.gg/announcing-the-voltaic-season-5-aiming-benchmarks-beta-for-kovaaks/)). Its season notes describe removing a jump bug and wall-interaction randomness judged unsuitable for benchmarking, adjusting score requirements, and resetting ranks, which is indirect evidence that score optimisation and threshold calibration are live problems. Coach opinion that mouse control is usually not the limiting factor is anecdote read from search summaries, and the Reddit threads on burnout and steep rank walls could not be retrieved, so those complaints remain uncited here.

On whether leaderboards themselves do harm, the evidence is thinner than the intuition. An experiment isolating points, levels and leaderboards found no effect on intrinsic motivation and an increase in quantity of output, not quality ([Mekler et al. 2017](https://glassmanlab.seas.harvard.edu/annotated_works/mekler17.pdf)). The supported claim is that leaderboards are not needed for motivation, and that Aim Lab's "compete" framing raises scores relative to "practice" framing ([Listman et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8720934)). Removing them is a defensible design choice that you should not describe as proven to improve learning.

| Claim | Evidence grade | Main caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Practice improves trainer scores for 60+ days | Strong for the trained task | Vendor-funded, one task, self-selected users |
| Trainer composite scores are reliable (ICC about 0.9+) | Strong | Raw hit rate is much less reliable |
| Better FPS players score better on aim tasks | Moderate, cross-sectional | Direction of causation unknown |
| Trainer practice improves in-game results | No controlled evidence | Open gap in the literature |
| Benchmark rank predicts game rank | No public data | Vendors may hold it privately |
| Fixed-scenario score chasing inflates apparent skill | Inference from specificity findings | Not directly quantified |

## Spacing and delayed testing hold up; most practice lore shrinks

### The short list of solid rules

Distributed practice beats massed practice, with a meta-analytic effect of about d = 0.46 overall and larger in motor subsets ([Donovan & Radosevich 1999](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/1999-donovan.pdf)). Performance during a session is an unreliable index of learning, and conditions that depress in-session scores often improve retention ([Soderstrom & Bjork 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25910388/)). The Aim Lab dataset fits both: performance rises quickly over the first few runs each day, only about 40 to 65% of a day's within-session gain carries to the next day, and skipped days cost little ([Listman et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8720934)). The dose figure from that paper should be quoted with care, because its abstract says 90% of the benefit arrives at 30 minutes per day while its Results section says about 49 one-minute runs, with maximum benefit near an hour. Match data from Halo and StarCraft 2 point the same way: breaks of a day or two did no harm ([Brown University release on Huang et al. 2017](https://www.brown.edu/news/2017-02-27/Gameskill)).

Three design consequences follow with reasonable confidence. Sessions of roughly 10 to 30 minutes on most days sit on the steep part of the only aim-specific dose curve; the principle is well supported and the exact minutes are extrapolated from one vendor-affiliated observational dataset. The first runs of a day, before warm-up, are the honest measure of learning, and last-run or best-run scores overstate it. And skill has to be read from the speed-accuracy trade-off across at least two difficulty levels, because a single speed or accuracy number confounds skill with strategy ([Donovan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9744923)).

### The longer list that weakens under scrutiny

Interleaved or random practice has a medium effect on delayed transfer in laboratory tasks (SMD 0.75) that falls to a non-significant 0.34 in applied settings ([Czyż et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11349744/)). Mouse aiming resembles the simple laboratory tasks where the effect is largest, so varying target size, distance, direction and speed within a session is a reasonable bet whose benefit for in-game aim is untested. External focus of attention drops to bias-corrected effects of g = 0.01 to 0.15 ([McKay et al. 2024](https://raw.github.com/cartermaclab/carterlab-papers/main/2024_mckay-etal_reporting-bias-not-external-focus-a-robust-bayesian-meta-analysis-and-systematic-review-of-the-external-focus-of-attention.pdf)), and a 43-volunteer Aim Lab experiment found no benefit of it either ([PMC10368276](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10368276)). Self-controlled practice falls from a naive g = 0.44 to a corrected 0.107 ([McKay et al. 2022](https://conferences.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2803)). Reduced feedback frequency shows no significant effect in meta-analysis ([preprint record](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.31234/osf.io/v2cp7)). Sleep-specific consolidation is reported at g = 0.43 and disputed as an artefact ([Schmid et al. 2020](https://boris.unibe.ch/146534); [Pan & Rickard 2015](https://zendy.io/pdf-viewer/10.1037/bul0000009)). Most of these numbers were read from abstracts and should be checked against the primary papers before being quoted as exact.

Player choice and attentional cues are therefore worth offering for enjoyment and adherence, and should not be presented as learning accelerators.

### What can ground a continuous growth curve

The often-quoted 85% success rule is a theoretical result for gradient-descent learners on binary classification, and its lead author limits it to that setting ([Wilson et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4); [University of Arizona News](https://news.arizona.edu/news/learning-optimized-when-we-fail-15-time)). The challenge-point framework says optimal difficulty rises with skill, and it remains mostly theoretical with no validated optimum ([scoping review](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/12281/1/ChallengeAcceptedASystematicScopingReviewOfTheapplicationsOfTheChallengePointFrameworkPV-JONES.pdf)). Aim Lab players do self-settle near 85 to 90% hit rate, which is suggestive and not causal. No study manipulates success rate in mouse aiming and measures retention, so any band, including the 70 to 90% proposed below, is a design choice.

What does have a methodological basis is the machinery for keeping a player at a chosen difficulty and reading their skill from it. Psychophysical staircases converge on a fixed success rate, with the convergence point set mainly by the ratio of down-step to up-step size ([García-Pérez 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(97)00340-4)). Bayesian methods estimate a threshold in fewer than 30 trials but need about 300 for slope and assume the player is not changing during the run ([Palamedes documentation of the psi method](https://www.palamedestoolbox.org/pal_ampm_demo.html)). None of these has been applied to mouse aiming in print. The continuous curve you want comes from making the staircase level itself the progress metric: a threshold in physical units ("target size you hit 80% of the time at this time budget") moves smoothly, has no rank boundaries, and cannot be raised by slowing down if the time budget is fixed.

Plateaus deserve a specific message to the player. Individual learning curves are steep early and slow later, and plateaus typically end when the performer changes method, often after a temporary dip ([Gray & Lindstedt 2017](https://api.philpapers.org/rec/GRAPDA); [Heathcote et al. 2000](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-power-law-repealed-the-case-for-an-exponential-law-of-3ur3vq1jx1.pdf)). Applying this to aiming is extrapolation, but it argues for changing the constraint on a plateau and warning the player that a dip is expected.

For choosing what to practise next with five to twenty users, education research offers methods that need little data. Elo-style ratings treat each attempt as a match between learner and item and need few parameters ([Pelánek 2016](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf)). The ZPDES bandit proposes whichever activity currently yields the fastest learning progress within an expert-defined graph, and it improved learning against a hand-designed curriculum in a 265-child trial ([Clément et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01669v2)). Item-response calibration and deep models need hundreds of learners and are impractical here. None of these has been used in aim training.

### Analytics and "AI coaching" rest on diagnosis, and the diagnosis is partly validated

Raw mouse deltas support a well-grounded feature set. Classical models describe an aimed movement as a fast primary submovement followed by optional corrections ([LJMU summary of Meyer et al. 1988](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/15895/3/Optimization%20in%20manual%20aiming%20relating%20inherent%20variability%20and%20target%20size%2C%20and%20its%20influence%20on%20tendency.pdf)). The Aim Lab kinematic study extracted reaction time, peak speed, signed landing error (undershoot or overshoot), precision and click timing relative to the movement, and found that shorter reaction time, less mid-swipe clicking and higher precision go with higher skill in professionals ([Donovan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9744923)). Click timing is modelled as a decision separate from pointing ([Park & Lee](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.02973)), so a miss can be a pointing error or a timing error.

The step from feature to advice is the weak link. Published norms cover professionals only, the parsing of corrective submovements is contested ([Roberts et al. 2024](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/22512/1/s00221-024-06784-0.pdf)), and when CS:GO professionals were instrumented against amateurs, only 6 of 13 community conjectures about what distinguishes pros were statistically supported, a result seen only as a search snippet ([DGIST record](https://dgist.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/secrets-of-gosu-understanding-physical-combat-skills-of-professio/)). No independent evidence shows that any commercial AI coach improves skill beyond practice itself. Aimlabs sells coaching review, adaptive difficulty and a sensitivity finder in a tier of about $10 a month ([Steam store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/714010/Aimlabs/)), and its algorithms are unpublished. A private trainer can beat that on transparency: show the data behind each statement, treat each piece of advice as a hypothesis, and record whether the prescribed drill moved the metric.

Detecting real improvement needs a noise model. Three to five one-minute runs give day-to-day reliability near 0.9 for hits per second and only 0.62 to 0.86 for hit rate ([Listman et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8720934)). Sports science supplies the individual-level framework: estimate each athlete's typical error from repeated tests and call a change real only when it exceeds that noise and a smallest worthwhile change ([Hopkins 2004](https://sportsci.org/jour/04/wghtests.htm)).

## Sphere targets drop the perceptual half of a gunfight

### Whether trainer visuals were designed for learning

No stated design rationale from KovaaK's or Aimlabs for flat, bright visuals was found, so "easy to build" cannot be confirmed or ruled out. The rationales that are on record concern measurement: NVIDIA's open-source FPSci cites parametric control and frame-wise logging ([NVIDIA Research](https://research.nvidia.com/index.php/publication/2022-08_firstpersonscience-open-source-tool-studying-fps-esports-aiming)), and Voltaic's choices reduce randomness and standardise FOV. Abstract visuals are good for measuring a motor act cleanly. Whether they are good for learning to win fights is a separate question that no aim-training study has tested.

Simulation research gives the frame for answering it. Reviews report that look-alike physical fidelity relates minimally to transfer and that functional alignment with the task matters more ([Reconsidering Fidelity in Simulation-Based Training](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259846424_Reconsidering_Fidelity_in_Simulation-Based_Training)). Psychological fidelity, the degree to which a simulator evokes the same sensory and cognitive processes as the real environment, is argued to be essential regardless of graphics ([A Psychological Fidelity Approach](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267562279_A_Psychological_Fidelity_Approach_to_Simulation-Based_Training_Theory_Research_and_Principles)). Applied to aiming, a sphere trainer has high functional fidelity for the motor step when sensitivity, FOV, target angular size and latency match the game, and close to none for finding and identifying a target. The evidence therefore supports controllable, task-relevant visual difficulty (contrast, clutter, occlusion, humanoid silhouettes, game-like backgrounds) over photorealism. That is an inference from general simulation literature, and it lowers the art and engine bar considerably.

Researchers also list what trainers strip out beyond visuals: cognitive load, moving targets with multiple aim points, simultaneous character movement, and outcomes that depend on teammates and opponents ([Warburton et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10411933/); [Rogers et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10925653/)). Recoil, counter-strafing, pre-aim and threat pressure belong on the same list by design reasoning, although no study quantifies how much each contributes.

### "I didn't see him" has separable, well-studied causes

Laboratory, radiology and baggage-screening research identifies several distinct ways to miss a visible target. None has been measured inside a commercial FPS, so the mapping to deaths in Warzone or Battlefield is extrapolation. Several of the classic papers below were cited from the researcher's memory without being re-fetched, and their numbers should be spot-checked.

Targets away from fixation are hard to resolve in clutter; crowding is described as peripheral vision's vulnerability to clutter, with a rule of thumb that objects closer together than about half their eccentricity interfere, a rule later reviews contest ([Pelli & Tillman 2008](https://www.cns.nyu.edu/~msl/courses/2223/Readings/Pelli-NatNeurosci2008.pdf); [Nature Reviews Psychology review](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s44159-022-00097-1)). Search slows as targets resemble their surroundings (recalled: [Duncan & Humphreys 1989](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.96.3.433)). Rare targets are missed more, with miss rates of about 0.46 at 2% prevalence against 0.20 at 50% as summarised by a secondary source ([prevalence effect summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prevalence_effect)). Finding one target makes a second less likely to be found ([Cognitive Research 2017](https://cognitiveresearchjournal.springeropen.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s41235-017-0072-5)). Attention committed elsewhere blinds even experts: 83% of 24 radiologists missed a gorilla inserted into a chest scan ([Radiology Today summary of Drew et al. 2013](https://www.radiologytoday.net/archive/rt0421p22.shtml)). And observers look where scene context says a person should be, which a model combining saliency, target features and context predicted across 912 outdoor scenes ([Ehinger et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2790194)). Off-angles exploit that last mechanism directly.

Camouflage research sharpens the hard case. For static targets every camouflage pattern lengthened response times, while for moving targets pattern had no effect and responses were roughly tenfold faster; identification of a moving camouflaged target among similar moving distractors stayed impaired ([Hall et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3619462)). For camouflaged soldiers, motion onset was the feature that captured attention ([Journal of Perceptual Imaging 2021 listing](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/adaptive-camouflage-of-moving-targets/)). A trainer in which every target pops in or strafes trains the easy case. The static, partly occluded, low-contrast holder is where training value should be highest.

### Camouflage-breaking is trainable, within similar scenery

This is the strongest support for your sixth concern, and it carries a firm limit. Fourteen sessions of camouflage-detection training in natural scenes left trained groups better than controls up to **three months later, but only for backgrounds and targets similar to those trained**; the authors recommend training on stimuli like the operational environment ([Acta Psychologica 2021 record](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/doaj.art-b33ad66db74443bd8119e6d893728f4b)). Earlier work found that trained observers improved on unseen instances and novel targets within a scene type, consistent with learning background statistics; its sample size and effect sizes sit behind a paywall and were not retrieved ([Chen & Hegdé 2012](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797612445315/)). The two results reconcile if learning attaches to a background family: it generalises across targets within that family and weakly across families. That reconciliation is an inference.

Neighbouring applied fields agree on structure. In X-ray baggage screening, computer-based training transfers to novel threat items, training with high category diversity improves detection of novel targets, and training mostly improves recognition and decision, not scanning ([review, PMC6647488](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6647488)). Exposure to backgrounds and distractors alone raised detection sensitivity in simulated X-ray search ([Cognitive Research 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41235-021-00295-0)). Hazard-perception training for drivers yields pooled gains of g = 0.78 on hazard-perception tests, with on-road translation still thin ([Prabhakharan et al. 2024 record](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/51376)). Perceptual learning generalises better with variable stimuli and multiple locations and tasks, and worse after prolonged training at a single threshold condition ([Xiong et al. 2016](https://elifesciences.org/articles/14614v2)).

General "vision improvement" claims do not survive. A critical review of NeuroTracker found only three studies testing far transfer to realistic tasks, two of them null ([Vater et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-021-01892-2)). Action-game training effects on cognition are estimated at g = 0.34 by proponents ([Bediou et al. 2018 record](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:101941)) and as minimal after bias correction by critics ([Hilgard et al. 2019 record](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/overestimation-of-action-game-training-effects-publication-bias-a/)). The defensible claim for a recognition mode is practice of the specific perceptual decisions a game family demands, with measurement, over ten to thirty sessions.

Expert eye-tracking evidence is small-sample and partly preprint, but it converges on a useful drill constraint: professionals spent more time looking at screen centre while amateurs looked more at the radar ([arXiv 1908.06403](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.06403)), and high-skill Valorant players had narrower horizontal gaze distributions ([arXiv 2310.15542](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.15542)). Detection should therefore be trained at controlled eccentricities while the player holds a central task, not by free scanning of a still image.

### Why this matters more for Call of Duty and Battlefield

Game snapshots support the contrast you drew, with the warning that they come mostly from data sites and guides and shift every season. Tactical shooters reward pre-aimed first-shot precision at predictable, high-contrast locations. Call of Duty and Battlefield combine short multi-bullet kill times, about 200 to 300 ms close-range for automatic primaries in Battlefield 6 by EA's own account ([EA gunplay post](https://www.ea.com/games/battlefield/battlefield-6/news/bf-combat-gunplay)) and roughly 190 to 310 ms for assault rifles in Black Ops 7 ([CODMunity](https://codmunity.gg/ttk/bo7)), with fast movement and wide adjustable FOV. Geometry makes wide FOV a visibility cost: on-screen target size scales with 1/tan(FOV/2), so going from 103 to 120 degrees horizontal shrinks central targets to about 73% of their linear size. Visibility has been a documented problem in Warzone, where the all-black Roze skin was adjusted twice by the developer ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/call-of-duty-warzone-skin-had-to-be-nerfed-twice-1847148667)). The standard benchmark taxonomy of clicking, tracking and switching contains no detection category, and no surveyed product advertises detection training in realistic scenes. That last point comes from store pages and was not independently audited.

No peer-reviewed experiment was found on FOV, stretched resolution, digital vibrance, colour filters, motion blur or anti-aliasing and target detection. A trainer that measures detection thresholds per user under their own settings would be producing data nobody has published.

## Sensitivity is a plateau; latency is the hard constraint

### The evidence favours a range

The only controlled FPS sensitivity study tested 13 experienced players across 5 to 160 cm per 360-degree turn and found a **plateau from 20 to 80 cm/360** (0.45 to 1.8 degrees per mm) where adjacent levels did not differ, with clear degradation outside it ([Boudaoud et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.12050)). Low sensitivity slowed the first ballistic submovement; high sensitivity added corrective submovements. Harder targets (small or distant) favoured lower sensitivity and easy targets higher. The study is NVIDIA-authored, used static targets only at a single FOV, and reports no effect sizes. The best pointing study shows the same shape for cursor gain: an L-shaped curve where low gain hurts through limb-speed limits and clutching, and high gain costs little until targets approach the hand's roughly 0.2 mm precision ([Casiez et al. 2008](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/design/2008-casiez.pdf)). Its bounds, largest movement over about 30 cm of usable travel and smallest target over hand resolution, can be computed per player, though pointer gain does not convert numerically to view rotation.

Professional settings sit inside the plateau. Published configurations for 816 CS2 professionals give a median eDPI of 800, which converts to about 52 cm/360, with an interquartile range of roughly 43 to 59 cm ([csdb.gg](https://csdb.gg/data/pro-sensitivity/)). These are descriptive config data. Figures for Valorant, Apex and Overwatch come from low-authority aggregator pages, and no reliable 2026 medians were found for Call of Duty or Battlefield.

"Never change your sensitivity" is not supported. After cycling through six sensitivities spanning a 32-fold range in about 80 minutes, players showed no significant degradation at their preferred setting, most performed better at one of the presets than at their habitual value, and the authors discarded the first 250 of 500 trials at each new setting as adaptation ([Boudaoud et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.12050)). A change within the plateau costs minutes on a simple flick task. Long-term switching and deliberate sensitivity randomisation are untested for FPS aiming.

Feel-based finders fail for identifiable reasons. Users of the "Perfect Sensitivity Approximation" bisection report different results on repeated runs and dependence on warm-up ([mouse-sensitivity.com forum](https://mouse-sensitivity.com/forums/topic/6013-need-a-sensitivity-finding-method-that-isnt-psa)). Bisection on a flat, noisy plateau, judged by comfort while the player is still adapting, cannot converge reproducibly. No validated FPS sensitivity-finding procedure has been published, and no documentation of Aim Lab's finder was found. The nearest precedent is AutoGain, a preprint that individualised a desktop pointing gain function from per-submovement overshoot and undershoot in under half an hour ([arXiv 1611.08154](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.08154v2)).

Mouse acceleration has no controlled FPS evidence either way; in desktop pointing it was 3.3% faster overall with more overshoot ([Casiez et al. 2008](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/design/2008-casiez.pdf)). Hardware effects are small: sensor position is worth about 4% of throughput ([Kim et al. 2020](https://userinterfaces.aalto.fi/mouse_sensor_position)), and polling above 1000 Hz has no controlled performance evidence.

### FOV is geometry with no performance data

No controlled study relates FOV to mouse aiming or detection in desktop FPS. What exists is exact projection math: on-screen size and on-screen speed both scale with 1/tan(FOV/2), and sensitivity matching across FOVs is a choice between methods that each preserve one thing. Focal-length scaling preserves hand distance per on-screen pixel near the crosshair; 360-distance matching preserves hand distance per world angle. Community practice favours focal-length scaling for precision, and none of the methods has been compared empirically ([mouse-sensitivity.com forum](https://mouse-sensitivity.com/forums/topic/8227-yet-another-monitor-distance-formula-variant)).

The practical consequence is that FOV is something to match exactly for transfer, and to explore only as a measured visibility trade-off in the recognition mode. Conversion needs each game's yaw constant and FOV convention. The yaw values for CS2 and Apex (0.022), Valorant (0.07), and Overwatch 2 and Call of Duty (0.0066) are consistent across a converter site and the NVIDIA paper's own table ([csdb.gg converter](https://csdb.gg/sensitivity-converter/)). Fortnite and Rainbow Six Siege have conflicting or single sources, and per-game ADS multipliers and FOV conventions for Call of Duty, Battlefield and Apex were not verified. Every game preset needs a side-by-side cm/360 check in the real game before it is trusted.

### Latency is well evidenced and bounds the platform choice

End-to-end latency has a large, replicated effect on aiming. With eight ranked players, latency produced large effects on flicking and tracking completion time while refresh rate mattered mostly through the latency it removes ([Spjut et al. 2019](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2019-11_Latency-of-30/FPS_Tasks_sa2019_AuthorVersion.pdf)). In an NVIDIA experiment run inside KovaaK's with more than 15,000 players, mean scores at 25 ms were about 24% above those at 85 ms on one task and roughly double on a hard flicking task ([NVIDIA developer blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/improving-player-performance-with-low-latency-as-evident-from-fps-aim-trainer-experiments/)). Both are vendor sources with trainer-internal outcomes, but the direction is consistent across studies. A trainer that adds a frame or two relative to the target game biases scores and any sensitivity recommendation, and scores are comparable across sessions only if frame pacing is stable.

## The recommended design and build path

The design below is assembled from the evidence above. Its components have support of varying strength in their home fields, the specific numbers are starting points, and nothing about the whole has been validated.

### Training modes built from game profiles

Define a game profile as editable data: yaw, FOV convention and value, ADS FOV and sensitivity multiplier, movement speeds, health and fire rate, and a visual "biome" set. Define scenarios as data files that specify targets in world metres, distance and motion model, so the profile's FOV determines angular size. Randomise spawn positions, sizes, distances and motion parameters on every run so that no pattern can be memorised; this trades a little measurement cleanliness for resistance to scenario-specific learning.

Keep the motor modes separate, because specificity argues against expecting flick practice to improve tracking: static and dynamic clicking, precise and reactive tracking, and target switching, using Voltaic's taxonomy as a design vocabulary. It is practitioner convention and has not been validated psychometrically. Add two composite modes tied to game style. The tactical composite presents a peek at head height near a pre-aimed position and rewards a small correction and a first accurate shot. The Call of Duty and Battlefield composite chains detection, an ADS snap, and tracking a strafing humanoid through a kill time of several bullets.

Build the recognition mode as a psychophysics task in game-like scenery. Log per trial the target-background contrast computed from the rendered frame, local clutter, the visible fraction of the silhouette, eccentricity from the crosshair in degrees, angular size, exposure duration, motion state and whether a target was present. Score three stages separately: detect (present or absent, or click the location), identify (friend or foe), and acquire (time from detection to first hit), so slow aim is never read as poor perception. Include target-absent trials and score with sensitivity and criterion measures (recalled reference: [Stanislaw & Todorov 1999](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03207704)), so that clicking at everything does not look like improvement. The notes suggest 30 to 50% absent trials in assessment blocks and a separate rare-threat block; those figures have no evidence base. The drills map onto the miss mechanisms: holding a central angle while detecting peripheral peeks, sweeping a scene containing zero to two static partly hidden figures, skins matched to backgrounds at graded contrast, a large library of small exposures over cover edges, targets at off-angles against scene expectations, brief friend-or-foe flashes, detection while reading a HUD prompt, and background-only familiarisation of a biome. Within training blocks, vary backgrounds and locations and keep a share of clearly visible trials; hold out scenes that are never trained for assessment.

### Measurement and transfer checking

Start each session with a short fixed probe before any warm-up, at fixed difficulty and with no coaching overlays, always framed the same way. Report medians of several runs with a spread measure, never personal bests. Convert everything to physical units (centimetres on the pad, degrees on screen) so data survive setting changes, and treat a sensitivity or hardware change as a break in the baseline. After five to eight sessions, compute each player's own session-to-session noise for each metric and declare improvement only when a smoothed estimate moves well beyond it and stays there. A simple latent-skill model with a random walk supports statements such as "likely improved since October". Show progress as each player's own threshold curves with noise bands, plus cooperative group goals if friends want a social layer. No ranks, no cross-player leaderboard by default.

Check transfer as a ladder of outcomes: trained scenarios; held-out parameter variants; structurally different probes administered rarely so they are not themselves trained; a standardised in-game test such as a fixed range or bot drill; and real-match statistics over many matches. Shrinking gains down the ladder quantify specificity. For in-game outcomes prefer statistics less dependent on teammates than kill-death ratio or rank, such as headshot percentage, accuracy by range and opening-duel results, and collect two to four weeks of baseline before training begins. With a handful of users, use within-person designs: staggered starts, alternating train and withhold blocks, or training one skill family while holding another as an untrained control and checking that the matching in-game statistic moves selectively. Log game and trainer time so trainer minutes can be weighed against the same minutes of deathmatch. Name the primary metric in advance. The test-retest reliability of these in-game statistics and the number of matches needed are unknown, and whether each game's data access permits this was not researched.

### Adaptive methods

Run one staircase per skill and parameter, adapting a single dimension per block (target size at a fixed time budget, target speed, or exposure duration). To hold a target success rate p with a one-up, one-down rule, make the easier step p/(1-p) times the harder step, which is about 5.7 times at 85%; the step ratio sets the convergence point. Clamp the change per step so difficulty never jumps, and show the level to the player as their metric, which addresses the known complaint that hidden difficulty adjustment undermines a sense of competence ([Fisher & Kulshreshth 2025](https://www.intechopen.com/online-first/1228576)). Hold novices nearer 85 to 90% and push experienced players toward 70 to 80% on the sub-skill being trained, as a hypothesis to revisit with your own retention data. Fit thresholds offline from the accumulated trial log, re-initialising each session with widened uncertainty because learners are not stationary.

Select drills with a learning-progress bandit over a hand-authored skill graph, and let the player choose among its top suggestions. Diagnose weak spots relative to the player's own baseline across conditions, for example undershoot that grows with flick distance, or a direction with worse endpoint scatter. Compute split-half reliability of kinematic features on your own data before coaching from them, since their reliability is unpublished.

### Settings discovery procedure

First bound the search space from pad width and the smallest targets the player's game demands, clipped to roughly 15 to 90 cm/360. Second, diagnose passively at the current sensitivity from ordinary training data: signed first-submovement error by flick amplitude, corrective submovement count, first-submovement peak speed, lift frequency and tracking error. Persistent overshoot with many corrections on small targets points to too high; a slow ballistic phase, undershoot on large flicks or frequent lifts points to too low. Third, test actively only when diagnosis suggests a move, alternating blocks of the current setting and one candidate about 1.25 to 1.5 times away, discarding the early part of each block. Fourth, fit a smooth curve over log sensitivity to a combined speed-accuracy score and report the range within noise of the best value. Fifth, fit flicking, small corrections and tracking separately and weight them by the player's game. Sixth, require the advantage to replicate on another day. The output reads: your data are consistent with X to Y cm/360, you are at Z, and the evidence for moving is weak, moderate or strong. Small differences needed hundreds to thousands of trials to detect in latency research ([Spjut et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.10498)), so "no detectable difference" will be a common and correct answer.

Handle FOV in two parts. For motor training, match the target game's FOV exactly. For visibility, let the recognition mode measure the player's detection thresholds at two or three FOV values and with their in-game colour settings, which turns a matter of lore into a personal measurement.

### The role of an LLM

Confine the LLM to language. The pipeline runs from raw telemetry through deterministic feature extraction and a statistical layer to a compact player profile containing thresholds with uncertainty, flagged deviations, trial counts and trend status; the LLM turns that profile into a session note and a plan. Evidence from health coaching supports grounding in aggregated user data: a Gemini-based model was evaluated on textualised sensor summaries, not raw streams ([PH-LLM, arXiv 2406.06474](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.06474v1)). The guardrails are a closed drill catalogue selected by structured output, a check that rejects any number or metric absent from the profile, wording rules that pass uncertainty through and allow "no reliable change yet", a developer-written advice library the model selects from, and self-referenced comparisons only. Keep a template-based fallback generated from the same profile. No evaluation of LLM coaching in esports was found, so compare template and LLM notes on your own group and record whether each recommendation moved its metric.

### Platform trade-offs and phases

| Option | Input and latency | Visual ceiling for cluttered scenes | Iteration with an AI assistant | Sharing | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Browser (Three.js or WebGPU) | Unaccelerated deltas in Chromium only; no control of presentation queue depth | Moderate | Fastest; all text | A URL | Unmeasured latency penalty |
| Godot 4.7.2 or later | Raw input on Windows; 8 kHz mouse fix shipped August 2026 | Good enough for controllable clutter | Strong; text project files | Zipped build | Fewer presentation controls than custom code |
| Unity 6 | Raw input through the Input System | High | Medium; editor-centric | Build | Heavier workflow |
| Custom native (SDL3, Rust) | Full control of input thread and swap chain | Whatever you build | Good, most code to write | Build | Scope |
| FPSci fork | Research-grade and latency-aware | Low; old engine | Poor | Windows build | Non-commercial share-alike licence |

Several cells are the notes' judgement and background knowledge, and Unreal was not researched. The sourced facts are these. Browsers can disable pointer acceleration with `unadjustedMovement` in Chromium ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/disable-mouse-acceleration)), but a practitioner test found no measurable latency benefit from the available browser flags and concluded that latency stays poor without queue-depth control ([Darpinian 2022](https://james.darpinian.com/blog/latency-platform-considerations/)); no rigorous browser-versus-native measurement exists. Godot versions before 4.7.2 collapsed to under one frame per second with an 8 kHz mouse in captured mode ([Godot blog](https://godotengine.org/article/fixing-high-polling-rate-mice-on-windows/)). On Windows the correct input path is Raw Input, with a known message-flood pitfall at high polling rates ([PH3 blog](https://ph3at.github.io/posts/Windows-Input/)). FPSci is licensed CC BY-NC-SA and is best used as a reference for config-driven sessions and frame-wise SQLite logging ([GitHub NVlabs/FPSci](https://github.com/NVlabs/FPSci)).

The recommendation is Godot 4.7.2 or later on Windows, with scenarios as data files, telemetry as append-only logs, and analysis in Python outside the game. It balances input fidelity, enough rendering for parameterised clutter, and fast iteration for one developer. Choose custom native only if the Phase 0 latency measurement shows Godot's presentation path is the limiting factor.

Phase 0 is a fidelity spike of a week or two: raw input with per-report timestamps, rotation as counts times yaw times sensitivity in double precision, hit tests at the click's timestamp instead of the frame boundary, an automated test that N counts produce the expected rotation, a cm/360 comparison against one real game, and a latency measurement on your own machine with your own mouse. Phase 1 adds one game profile, six to eight data-defined scenarios across the motor modes, full telemetry and the cold-probe session structure. Phase 2 adds the measurement layer (noise model, threshold curves, self-referenced progress views), staircases and the drill-selection bandit; start the in-game baseline window here. Phase 3 builds the recognition mode with one biome pack, procedural placement, signal-detection scoring and held-out scenes. Phase 4 adds the settings procedure, the LLM note with its template fallback, and the first within-person transfer check. Phase 5 extends to a second game profile and biome, and to friends' machines with per-machine latency logging.

### Open decisions for you

Platform comes first: accept the Godot recommendation, or prototype in the browser for speed and accept an unmeasured latency penalty. Which game to target first matters more than it looks, because it fixes the first profile, the first biome's art style, and the in-game statistics available for transfer checking. Your recognition concern points at a Call of Duty or Battlefield profile, yet those are the games whose FOV and ADS conventions were not verified and whose stat access was not researched; a tactical shooter is better documented for yaw and FOV and may be easier to measure. Decide how much burden you and your friends will tolerate for transfer checking, since baseline windows and withhold blocks cost enjoyment. Decide whether the LLM runs locally or through a cloud API fed only the profile. Decide how far to go on art: procedurally parameterised clutter with a few free assets is the scope-safe default, and realistic maps are the main route to scope creep. Finally, decide whether friends see each other's data at all, and if so whether only as cooperative group goals.

## Conclusion

The evidence changes what the product is. With no proof that any trainer transfers, a trainer's scenarios are hypotheses and its telemetry is the experiment; the most valuable feature is the ladder from trained drill to held-out probe to in-game statistic, because it tells each player whether their minutes are better spent here or in deathmatch. For some of your friends the honest answer will be that mechanical aim is not their bottleneck, and a tool that says so is doing its job.

The recognition mode is the one place where the design goes beyond what commercial trainers advertise, and it rests on real laboratory evidence with a clear boundary: learning follows the scenery. That turns art direction into a training variable and makes "which game first" the decision that shapes everything downstream. It also means the project can generate data no one has published, including per-user detection thresholds under FOV and colour settings, the reliability of kinematic features, and whether detection thresholds predict deaths to unseen enemies. At a scale of five to twenty people these will be case series, never proof, but they are enough to steer your own training.
