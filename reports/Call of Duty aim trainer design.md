# Train the whole gunfight, measure every stage

No controlled study shows that practice in any aim trainer improves in-game results, and nothing published covers aiming through an aim-down-sights (ADS) transition, recoil control, weapon clutter, or mouse turns toward a sound, so the honest design is a trainer that reproduces the Call of Duty gunfight as one timed chain and tests its own usefulness on you and your friends. What is empirically solid is narrower: practice improves trainer scores, trainer scores are reliable, practice spread across days beats massed practice, latency strongly affects aiming, sensitivity is a broad plateau, camouflage detection is trainable within similar scenery, and short active training with feedback cuts front-back sound confusions from about 41% with gains that last months. Call of Duty's own numbers explain why a click trainer misses the game: assault-rifle ADS time (225 to 300 ms) is as long as the time to kill (roughly 200 to 335 ms), so a fight is decided across cue, turn, detection, ADS and a held burst, and a single flick is a small part of it. The recommended design is therefore one engagement loop (cue, orient, detect, ADS while snapping, hold fire through a multi-bullet kill under recoil and clutter, reset to an unknown next target) with a timestamp at every stage, so the app can say which stage costs a player their fights and prescribe an isolated drill only for that stage. Long tracking and many-visible-target switching are dropped from the Call of Duty profile on design reasoning, since neither resembles a 200 to 335 ms kill against one enemy whose position cannot be planned. Nearly every Call of Duty value in this report is community-derived or read from search snippets, the audio and ADS notes rest on a preprint, abstract-only reads and several blocked sites, and every design number is a starting point, so the build path starts with side-by-side verification in the real game and with latency and audio-onset measurement on your own machine.

## Trainer practice has no proven in-game payoff, so the trainer must test itself

### What the trainer studies measured

Every located study using Aim Lab or KovaaK's data measures performance inside the trainer. The flagship paper analysed **7,174 Aim Lab players and 682,564 sixty-second Gridshot runs**; its outcomes were hit rate and hits per second, and the authors had no information on what games the players played ([Listman et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8720934)). It is observational, single-task and was funded and written by Statespace, the maker of Aim Lab. A follow-up on 32 professional and semi-professional players is kinematic and leaves the correlation of Aim Lab scores with in-game results as future work ([Donovan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9744923)). The one independent KovaaK's paper is a ten-player reliability pilot whose authors caution against extrapolating to in-game performance because the trainer isolates aiming while real play adds cognitive load ([Rogers et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10925653/)). A randomised study of 21 novices found that about 30 hours of CS:GO deathmatch against bots improved in-engine aim tests, which sets the bar a trainer must beat for the same minutes ([Neri et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8224404/)). Self-reported FPS players acquired targets faster than non-players, 588 ms against 727 ms, although gaming hours did not predict performance, so the cross-sectional pattern fits transfer, reverse causation and self-selection equally ([Warburton et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10411933/)).

Transfer science predicts gains that are largest on the trained task and shrink with dissimilarity. A review of brain-training studies found improvement on trained tasks and no compelling evidence of real-world benefit ([Simons et al. 2016, APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/brain-training.html)), far transfer "rarely occurs" across chess, music and working-memory training ([Sala & Gobet 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721417712760)), and a scoping review found positive motor transfer in only **36% of studies** ([JEMS scoping review](https://www.scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/2548)). Pulling the other way, first-person camera aiming and ordinary cursor pointing have nearly identical spatial kinematics, so raw mouse control looks like a fairly general capacity ([Warburton et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10411933/)). The reasonable reading, which is inference, is that basic pointing precision transfers somewhat and that score gains on a repeated fixed scenario overstate it.

Fixed benchmarks invite scenario-specific learning. In the Aim Lab data, median hits per second rose from **2.72 on day 1 to 3.78 on day 60** while hit rate moved only from 86.1% to 89.6%, so gains were mostly speed at near-constant accuracy ([Listman et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8720934)); Gridshot elicits its own strategy of clicking mid-movement, and one Fitts's-law model failed across two tasks differing mainly in target size ([Donovan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9744923)). No published correlation between Voltaic, KovaaK's or Aim Lab rank and competitive rank was found. The no-leaderboard decision is defensible and should not be described as proven to improve learning: an experiment isolating points, levels and leaderboards found more output and no change in intrinsic motivation or quality ([Mekler et al. 2017](https://glassmanlab.seas.harvard.edu/annotated_works/mekler17.pdf)).

### Practice rules that hold up, and those that shrink

Distributed practice beats massed practice, with a meta-analytic effect of about d = 0.46 ([Donovan & Radosevich 1999](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/1999-donovan.pdf)), and performance during a session is an unreliable index of learning ([Soderstrom & Bjork 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25910388/)). The Aim Lab data fit both: only about 40 to 65% of a day's within-session gain carries to the next day and skipped days cost little ([Listman et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8720934)). Three consequences follow with reasonable confidence: sessions of roughly 10 to 30 minutes on most days (principle well supported, minutes extrapolated from one vendor dataset); the first runs of a day, before warm-up, are the honest measure of learning; and skill must be read from the speed-accuracy trade-off across difficulty levels, since a single number confounds skill with strategy.

The popular rules weaken under scrutiny. Interleaved practice has a medium effect in laboratory tasks (SMD 0.75) that falls to a non-significant 0.34 in applied settings ([Czyż et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11349744/)). External focus of attention drops to bias-corrected effects of g = 0.01 to 0.15 ([McKay et al. 2024](https://raw.github.com/cartermaclab/carterlab-papers/main/2024_mckay-etal_reporting-bias-not-external-focus-a-robust-bayesian-meta-analysis-and-systematic-review-of-the-external-focus-of-attention.pdf)), and self-controlled practice falls from g = 0.44 to a corrected 0.107 ([McKay et al. 2022](https://conferences.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2803)). Most of these figures were read from abstracts. The 85% success rule is a theoretical result for a specific class of learner and its author limits it to that setting ([Wilson et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4)); no study manipulates success rate in mouse aiming, so any target band is a design choice. What has a methodological basis is the machinery: psychophysical staircases converge on a chosen success rate set by the ratio of step sizes ([García-Pérez 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(97)00340-4)), Elo-style ratings need few parameters ([Pelánek 2016](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf)), and a learning-progress bandit over an expert-defined activity graph beat a hand-designed curriculum in a 265-child trial ([Clément et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01669v2)). None has been applied to aim training in print.

Diagnosis from mouse data is partly validated. An aimed movement is a fast primary submovement plus optional corrections; the Aim Lab kinematic study extracted reaction time, peak speed, signed landing error, precision and click timing and found shorter reaction time and higher precision in more skilled professionals ([Donovan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9744923)). The step from feature to advice is weak, and no independent evidence shows any commercial AI coach improves skill beyond practice. Detecting real change needs a noise model: three to five one-minute runs give day-to-day reliability near 0.9 for hits per second and only 0.62 to 0.86 for hit rate ([Listman et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8720934)), and sports science supplies the rule of calling a change real only when it exceeds the individual's typical error and a smallest worthwhile change ([Hopkins 2004](https://sportsci.org/jour/04/wghtests.htm)).

### Latency binds hard, sensitivity is a plateau, FOV is geometry

End-to-end latency has a large, replicated effect on aiming. With eight ranked players, latency produced large effects on flicking and tracking time ([Spjut et al. 2019](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2019-11_Latency-of-30/FPS_Tasks_sa2019_AuthorVersion.pdf)), and in an NVIDIA experiment inside KovaaK's with more than 15,000 players, mean scores at 25 ms were about 24% above those at 85 ms on one task and roughly double on a hard flicking task ([NVIDIA developer blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/improving-player-performance-with-low-latency-as-evident-from-fps-aim-trainer-experiments/)). Both are vendor sources with trainer-internal outcomes. A trainer that adds a frame or two relative to the game biases every stage timing proposed below.

The only controlled FPS sensitivity study, with 13 players, found a **plateau from 20 to 80 cm per 360-degree turn** where adjacent levels did not differ, and players showed no significant degradation at their preferred setting after cycling through six sensitivities ([Boudaoud et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.12050)). The same authors state that scoped or zoomed sensitivity was excluded and is future work ([arXiv 2203.12050](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.12050)). The right output of any settings procedure is therefore a range with a strength-of-evidence label, and no research supports any particular ADS sensitivity scheme. No controlled study relates FOV to aiming or detection; what exists is exact projection math, in which on-screen target size scales with 1/tan(FOV/2), so going from 103 to 120 degrees shrinks central targets to about 73% of their linear size.

| Claim | Evidence grade | Main caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Practice improves trainer scores for 60+ days | Strong for the trained task | Vendor-funded, one task |
| Spacing practice across days helps; in-session scores mislead | Strong, general motor learning | Dose in minutes is extrapolated |
| Latency degrades aiming | Strong direction, vendor data | Outcomes are trainer-internal |
| Sensitivity is a plateau of 20 to 80 cm/360 | One study, 13 players, hipfire only | Scoped sensitivity untested |
| Camouflage detection is trainable for months | Moderate, laboratory | Only for similar backgrounds |
| Training cuts front-back sound confusions | Moderate, small studies, one a preprint read | Research renderers, not game audio |
| Trainer practice improves in-game results | No controlled evidence | Open gap |
| ADS transition, recoil, clutter, mouse-turn-to-sound | No FPS studies found | Everything here is extrapolation |

## Seeing the enemy is a separate, trainable skill bounded by scenery

Sphere trainers keep the motor step and drop the perceptual one. Simulation reviews report that look-alike fidelity relates minimally to transfer and that functional alignment with the task matters more ([Reconsidering Fidelity in Simulation-Based Training](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259846424_Reconsidering_Fidelity_in_Simulation-Based_Training)), which supports controllable visual difficulty (contrast, clutter, occlusion, humanoid silhouettes) over photorealism. That is inference from general simulation literature, and it keeps the art bar low.

The causes of "I didn't see him" are separable in laboratory work, though none has been measured inside a commercial shooter. Peripheral targets are hard to resolve in clutter ([Pelli & Tillman 2008](https://www.cns.nyu.edu/~msl/courses/2223/Readings/Pelli-NatNeurosci2008.pdf)); attention committed elsewhere blinds even experts, as when 83% of 24 radiologists missed a gorilla inserted into a chest scan ([Radiology Today summary of Drew et al. 2013](https://www.radiologytoday.net/archive/rt0421p22.shtml)); and observers look where scene context says a person should be ([Ehinger et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2790194)). Camouflage patterns lengthen response times for static targets while moving targets are found roughly tenfold faster ([Hall et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3619462)), so a trainer in which every target pops in or strafes trains the easy case.

Detection is trainable with a firm boundary. Fourteen sessions of camouflage-detection training left trained groups better than controls up to **three months later, but only for backgrounds and targets similar to those trained** ([Acta Psychologica 2021 record](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/doaj.art-b33ad66db74443bd8119e6d893728f4b)). In baggage screening, training transfers to novel threat items and benefits from category diversity ([review, PMC6647488](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6647488)), and perceptual learning generalises better with variable stimuli and locations ([Xiong et al. 2016](https://elifesciences.org/articles/14614v2)). General vision-training claims do not survive: a review of NeuroTracker found only three far-transfer studies, two of them null ([Vater et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-021-01892-2)). Visibility has been a documented Call of Duty problem, with the all-black Roze skin adjusted twice by the developer ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/call-of-duty-warzone-skin-had-to-be-nerfed-twice-1847148667)). For this design, detection becomes one timed stage of the engagement loop, performed in clutter that resembles the game, with the weapon model and firing effects added as a second clutter source that the earlier analysis did not consider.

## Sound gives the hemisphere, the turn resolves front from back

### What headphone localisation research shows

Reliability of this section: one key paper was read as a preprint whose published version was not opened; several findings come from abstracts or search snippets only; the Nature, Acta Acustica and callofduty.com pages could not be fetched. Snippet-only items are flagged and should be treated as leads.

Left-right judgement over headphones is robust and front-back judgement with a generic head-related transfer function (HRTF) is poor. Untrained listeners using a non-individual HRTF with the head still showed a lateral error of about 15 degrees, a spherical error of about 43 degrees and a **front-back confusion rate of about 41%** (n = 36; preprint read in full, published version in Scientific Reports not read directly) ([Steadman et al., bioRxiv v3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/207753v3.full)). A full-text peer-reviewed study found lateral error of 17 to 18 degrees with each listener's worst-rated HRTF against 11 degrees with their own, with far larger front-back error; it deliberately used the worst HRTF, so it overstates the penalty of a typical generic one ([Stitt, Picinali & Katz 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6355836/)). Distance judgement is generally poorer than direction (snippet-only) ([Kolarik et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4744263)), and elevation over generic HRTFs is weak enough that above-or-below should not become a headline metric.

The finding that matters most for a mouse game is that listener-controlled movement of the sound scene resolves front-back ambiguity. With the head still, confusions disappeared when the listener moved the source with key presses and not when the experimenter moved it (abstract only) ([Wightman & Kistler 1999](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/861554450)); small yaw rotations below 10 degrees substantially reduce confusions (snippet-only, n = 7) ([McLachlan et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2023.1027827/full)). A mouse turn is a listener-controlled rotation, so turning the camera should substitute for turning the head, provided the sound is still playing during the turn and the renderer updates quickly. A footstep train supplies that; a single step that ends before the turn does not. This is extrapolation: no study tests mouse rotation.

Training works, quickly, for most people. In the Steadman study, nine 12-minute blocks over three days with position feedback cut spherical error by about 18 degrees and **front-back confusions by about 15 percentage points**, with half the gain arriving in about 19 minutes; game dressing alone added nothing, and "active listening", where the sound looped while the listener moved, gave the largest gains ([Steadman et al.](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/207753v3.full)). Improvement carried to a second, untrained HRTF and an untrained sound. Stitt and colleagues found front-back error falling over four weekly 12-minute sessions and holding for three months, improvement rates ranging from 0.5 to 4.6 degrees per session, and one listener who never resolved front-back confusions ([Stitt et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6355836/)). Passive exposure did nothing while short active training with feedback helped (abstract only) ([Mendonça et al. 2012](https://repositorium.sdum.uminho.pt/handle/1822/26405)), and gains generalised to untrained positions and sounds a month later (abstract only) ([Mendonça et al. 2013](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/12865609/journal.pone.0077900.pdf)). The trainable deficit is front-back resolution; lateral judgement is already good and showed little learning.

Transfer to Call of Duty is plausible and unproven. All of these studies used anechoic rendering of broadband sounds, while a game adds distance curves, occlusion, reverb and mixing. What most plausibly transfers is the strategy (use left-right first, turn to disambiguate front-back) more than a fine spectral map.

### Sound as a cue to turn, and why attention matters

Spatial sound shortens visual search, most for targets outside the field of view. A click train co-located with a visual target cut search time by 175 to 1,200 ms (abstract only) ([Perrott et al. 1990](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~saberi/perrottetal1990.pdf)), and for out-of-view targets an audio cue that updated as the head moved beat a one-shot cue and matched a visual arrow (abstract only) ([Flanagan et al. 1998](https://scilit.com/publications/1eb24625dc9c5e431e805f3b46f356de)). FPS-specific evidence is thin: one paywalled conference study reports faster reactions with immersive audio than stereo, with sample and effect size unretrieved ([Brandmeyer et al.](https://www.bohrium.com/scholar/8w3703x7/Alex_Brandmeyer)), and nothing rigorous was found on footstep detection or on stereo against HRTF modes in Call of Duty. Audio precision is coarse, tens of degrees, so the realistic chain is that sound gives hemisphere and rough bearing, a fast coarse turn brings the target into view, and vision finishes. That chain is the reason the loop below scores the first turn separately from the final aim.

Missing a footstep is partly an attention failure. Under high visual load, failure to notice a tone reached 79% (abstract only) ([Macdonald & Lavie 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3152714)), and detection sensitivity itself fell, not only the response criterion (abstract only) ([Raveh & Lavie 2015](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1454101/1/art_10.3758_s13414-014-0776-2.pdf)). These studies used tones, and no study of training footstep detection in maskers was found. The extrapolated design consequence is that the cue for the next enemy should sometimes arrive while the player is still shooting the current one, and that footstep level and visual load must be manipulated separately.

### Call of Duty audio shifts every title and season

Footstep audibility is a tuning decision. Snippets of official notes show Warzone's Black Ops 6 integration raising enemy footstep volume and lowering the player's own ([Season 01 patch notes](https://callofduty.com/patchnotes/2024/11/call-of-duty-bo6-warzone-season-01-patch-notes)), and press reports describe Treyarch raising Black Ops 7 footstep volume after a beta in which footsteps were nearly undetectable ([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/call-of-duty/call-of-duty-black-ops-7-will-now-let-players-aim-down-sights-while-sliding-diving-and-wall-jumping-without-hated-dexterity-perk-and-its-also-adding-louder-footsteps)). Perks that silence footsteps mean some enemies give no cue at all. Since Black Ops 6 the game has offered an Embody Immerse HRTF mode with a free generic profile and a paid personalised one at USD 19.99, with no independent accuracy data; the same support page lists Modern Warfare 4 audio features ([Embody support](https://support.embody.co/portal/en/kb/articles/universal-vs-personalized-profiles-key-differences)). The in-game renderer is therefore never the same HRTF a trainer uses. Windows Loudness Equalization is widely recommended in guides to hear footsteps ([esports.gg guide](https://esports.gg/guides/call-of-duty/how-to-use-loudness-equalization-to-your-advantage-in-mw2-and-warzone-2)); it is dynamic range compression, and since level is a primary distance cue it should trade detection against distance judgement, which is reasoning and not a measurement. The durable skills are detection at low signal-to-noise, front-back resolution and the turn response, trained across a range of footstep levels, surfaces and HRTFs, with no-cue trials so players do not lean on a cue the game sometimes withholds.

## Call of Duty fights are decided inside the ADS window

### The numbers, and how far to trust them

Reliability of this section: mouse-sensitivity.com returned 403 on every fetch, callofduty.com patch notes and blogs reset or timed out, and no sym.gg, TrueGameData, XclusiveAce or JGOD page was read. Almost every Call of Duty number is either a search-snippet quote of official notes or comes from a single data site, CODMunity. Nothing was confirmed from in-game tooltips.

Three converter sites give the hipfire yaw as **0.0066 degrees per count** at sensitivity 1, and two published worked examples agree with it, for instance sensitivity 6 at 800 DPI giving about 28.9 cm/360 ([sensconverter.app](https://sensconverter.app/cod-sensitivity-converter/); [west-games calculator](https://west-games.com/cm-per-360-calculator/); [csdb.gg converter](https://csdb.gg/sensitivity-converter/)). No Activision document confirms it. The FOV slider is described as horizontal at 16:9 with a range of 60 to 120, and a published table (80 = 51 vertical, 120 = 89) matches the formula V = 2 atan(tan(H/2) x 9/16) to rounding ([esportstales](https://www.esportstales.com/call-of-duty/best-fov-field-of-view-modern-warfare-and-warzone)). One Reddit anecdote, that slider 107 on a 21:9 monitor is about 122 degrees, is consistent with the vertical angle staying fixed on other aspect ratios; one anecdote is not verification.

ADS sensitivity under the "Relative" setting follows a community-quoted monitor-distance formula on vertical FOV with a coefficient k: multiplier = atan(k tan(Vads/2)) / atan(k tan(Vhip/2)), known here only from search summaries of forum posts and a calculator page using the same expression ([calculator page](https://calculator.everycalculators.com/monitor-distance-coefficient-calculator/)). The default coefficient is reported as 1.33 ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1938090/discussions/0/4693406471885520449/)). Working the formula through shows the coefficient matters little for 1x sights (about a 15% spread across k from 0 to 1.78) and a lot at 4x (about 56%), so for rifles and SMGs the larger uncertainty is the ADS field of view itself. That value is unverified: a converter site lists irons and red dots at about 1.3x, and one guide says the "Independent" ADS FOV mode reverts to 65 at slider 120 ([Gfinity](https://www.gfinityesports.com/article/best-fov-settings-for-black-ops-6)). If 1.3x is defined against the old default of 80, the arithmetic gives 65.7, which matches, and it implies that Independent at slider 120 magnifies about 2.7 times on ADS while "Affected" magnifies 1.3 times. That is a plausible derivation, unconfirmed. The ADS sensitivity transition setting has three options described by guides and not by any official text: Instant applies the ADS multiplier on the button press, Gradual ramps it over the zoom, After Zoom applies it when the animation ends ([hone.gg](https://hone.gg/blog/ads-in-call-of-duty/)). What "Legacy" does is disputed among forum posters.

Handling and kill times come from one data site. For listed Black Ops 7 assault-rifle builds: **ADS 225 to 300 ms, sprint-to-fire 170 to 250 ms, time to kill at 20 m 204 to 312 ms**, fire rate 588 to 1,034 RPM, ADS movement 2.8 to 3.3 m/s ([CODMunity BO7](https://codmunity.gg/weapon-stats/bo7)). Black Ops 6 builds show ADS 230 to 300 ms and time to kill 200 to 335 ms, with time to kill defined from the first hit against 100 HP ([CODMunity BO6](https://codmunity.gg/weapon-stats/bo6)). Snippets of official notes confirm the units and the constant retuning, such as an ADS value moving from 245 to 240 ms in April 2026 ([Gfinity BO7 Season 3 notes](https://www.gfinityesports.com/article/black-ops-7-season-3-patch-notes)). No Modern Warfare 4 weapon data was retrieved; guides report a default FOV of 95 and a PC "Enhanced FOV" lens option ([Dexerto MW4](https://www.dexerto.com/wikis/modern-warfare-4/best-fov-settings/)).

Recoil and clutter are documented qualitatively and not numerically. Community measurement separates actual recoil, where bullets land, from visual recoil, the motion of gun, sight and camera, and holds that visual recoil grows with zoom and shrinks in apparent size at higher FOV ([MeinMMO](https://mein-mmo.de/en/cod-warzone-what-is-visual-recoil-and-why-is-it-more-important-than-the-real-recoil,781192)). Official notes tune "gun kick" and "view kick" separately and have repeatedly reduced visual recoil, for example an 18% cut on one weapon in April 2024 ([Comicbook.com](https://comicbook.com/gaming/news/call-of-duty-mw3-modern-warfare-update-patch-notes)). Black Ops 6 added a firing-range recoil wall and patterns are described as repeatable shapes ([Dexerto](https://dexerto.com/call-of-duty/black-ops-6-added-one-of-the-best-features-in-years-you-probably-missed-it-2962383)). Muzzle smoke and flash obscuring targets is a long-running complaint that developers have reduced several times, once without listing it in patch notes ([Sportskeeda](https://sportskeeda.com/call-of-duty-game/news-warzone-finally-changed-one-biggest-problems-game-two-years)), and guides recommend a Wide weapon FOV and the lowest camera-movement setting ([oneesports](https://www.oneesports.gg/call-of-duty/best-warzone-settings-bo6-guide/)). No recoil magnitudes, pattern coordinates or screen-coverage measurements were retrieved for any title.

### No research covers the transition, and no trainer simulates it

No peer-reviewed study was found on aiming through a zoom transition, scoped against unscoped aiming, weapon occlusion, muzzle flash, screen shake or recoil control. The nearest work, all read as search snippets, is on reaching: people can learn two visuomotor gains at once, more slowly than one ([Pearson, Krakauer & Mazzoni 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2888232)), and with a reliable cue switching between learned mappings becomes practically instantaneous after prolonged training ([PMC9217135](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9217135)). An ADS press is such a cue, which argues that the transition is trainable if practised with the real gains and timing. That is extrapolation from reaching studies. Nothing indicates which transition-timing option is best, so the trainer should measure aim error through the transition under the player's own setting and recommend nothing.

Existing trainers support zoom FOV and scoped sensitivity scaling. Aimlabs' Black Ops 7 material lists flicking, tracking and switching playlists and does not mention ADS, weapon models, recoil or hold-to-fire ([Aimlabs article](https://aimlabs.com/articles/aimlabs/black-ops-7-aim-training-playlists-you-should-be-using/)); Aiming.Pro added ADS as zoom FOV plus relative sensitivity and described the gun position change as aesthetic ([Aiming.Pro](https://aiming.pro/ads-now-available)). KovaaK's weapon editor and Aimlabs' in-app Call of Duty profile were not examined first-hand, and whether any trainer has an audio-cued mode was not checked, so "nobody does this" is supported by what was retrieved and not established.

### Why long tracking and many-target switching leave the profile

This is design reasoning, built on the single-data-site timings above. Hand sensorimotor delay is about 150 to 200 ms ([Spjut et al. 2019](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2019-11_Latency-of-30/FPS_Tasks_sa2019_AuthorVersion.pdf)). Add an ADS time of 225 to 300 ms and a time to kill of 200 to 335 ms, and the sequence from cue to dead target runs roughly 600 to 900 ms, of which the firing portion is the last third. A drill that tracks one target for tens of seconds practises steady-state closed-loop following that a Call of Duty kill window, one or two feedback delays long, never reaches; what the game demands instead is holding the crosshair on a strafing figure for under half a second while pulling against recoil at ADS sensitivity, and that is kept inside the loop. A drill that shows several targets at once lets the player plan the next movement before finishing the current one, while the user's own account of the game is one enemy at a time whose next position is unknown. Dropping both also removes the scenario types most open to memorised patterns. The cost is acknowledged: nothing shows that the engagement loop transfers better, and the specificity literature only makes it the better bet. Both drill families can remain available for other game profiles.

## One engagement loop, timed stage by stage

Every design number below is a starting point to tune against your own data.

### The loop and what each stage records

An engagement begins from a neutral state, with the player moving or holding an angle at hipfire, and a target that does not yet exist for them. The cue arrives at an unpredictable time, from an unpredictable direction including behind, in one of three forms: a sound (footstep train, with surface, level, masker, occlusion and HRTF logged), a brief visual glimpse, or nothing at all, in which case the target simply becomes visible somewhere. The player turns, finds the figure in clutter, presses ADS while snapping, holds fire through the number of bullets the weapon needs, and the loop resets with no information about where the next target will be. Trial schedules are generated from parameter distributions each session so no sequence repeats.

| Stage | Starts at | Ends at | What is logged | What a slow or poor stage indicates |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cue | Cue onset (latency-corrected for audio) | First mouse movement | Reaction time; detection and false alarms by footstep level (sensitivity and criterion) | Missed or late hearing; inattention under visual load |
| Orient | First movement | End of first ballistic turn | Turn side correct; hemisphere correct (front-back error rate); angular error before visual correction; time for target to enter FOV | Front-back confusion; undersized turns; slow large-angle mouse movement |
| Detect and identify | Target inside FOV | First movement toward it, or ADS press | Search time by contrast, clutter, visible fraction, eccentricity; friend-or-foe errors; target-absent false alarms | Visual search or identification, not aim |
| ADS and snap | ADS press | ADS complete | Order of ADS press against snap; aim error at press, through the zoom and at completion; overshoot or undershoot sign after the gain change; shots fired before ADS completed | Late ADS; error injected by the FOV and sensitivity change |
| Fire | First shot | Kill or failure | First-bullet hit; hit rate over the kill window; time to kill against the weapon's theoretical minimum; vertical and horizontal recoil residual; fraction of window with target covered by weapon, flash or smoke | Recoil control; tracking a strafe; losing the target behind clutter |
| Reset | Kill | Next cue | Recentering; ADS release; crosshair placement | Slow recovery; staying zoomed with a narrow view |

Because each stage has a start and end timestamp, the total time from cue to kill decomposes exactly, and the app can express each stage in two ways: against the player's own baseline for comparable trials, and against the weapon's floor (ADS time and theoretical time to kill cannot be beaten, so only the excess is the player's). A statement such as "your fights run 310 ms over the floor; 180 ms of that is between the target entering view and your ADS press, and it is worse on low-contrast trials" is the diagnostic output. Rear-hemisphere trials on which the first turn goes the wrong way are counted separately, since a front-back error costs a half-turn. Audio benefit is measured within-player as the difference between sound-cue and no-cue trials at matched target positions. Hipfire against ADS choice by target distance, with outcomes, estimates the player's own crossover range. All of these stage metrics are new measurements with unknown reliability, so split-half reliability on your own data comes before any coaching from them.

### Isolated drills only for a diagnosed stage

Isolated drills exist to repair the stage the loop flags, and the loop itself remains the main activity and the measure. For the orient stage, an audio localisation drill uses 10 to 12 minute blocks, a looping footstep source, a required turn response, and feedback showing the true position after each trial, progressing from left-right, to front-back in quiet, to front-back with maskers, to moving and occluded sources, to a concurrent visual task; these block lengths come from the HRTF training studies and everything else is extrapolation. A player whose front-back error stays flat after about an hour is flagged as a likely non-responder and pointed to a different HRTF set instead of more repetitions. For the detect stage, the recognition drills from the earlier design remain: static, partly occluded, low-contrast figures at controlled eccentricity while the player holds a central task, with target-absent trials. For the ADS stage, a transition drill presents a small-angle correction that must be completed through the zoom under the player's own transition setting and optic. For the fire stage, a recoil drill holds a burst on a static then strafing figure at ADS movement speeds, with clutter toggled on and off within a session to measure its cost. Short static and dynamic click drills at hipfire remain for a flagged first-shot problem. A drill is retained only if working on it moves its stage metric in the loop.

### Adaptation, measurement and transfer checking

Adapt one dimension per block with a staircase whose level is shown to the player as their metric in physical units: footstep signal-to-noise for detection, number of steps for localisation, contrast or exposure for visual detection, target strafe speed or recoil scale for firing. To hold a success rate p with a one-up, one-down rule, the easier step is p/(1-p) times the harder step, about 5.7 times at 85%. Choose which drill to offer with a learning-progress bandit over a hand-authored graph of the six stages, and let the player pick among its top suggestions.

Start each session with a short fixed probe of the loop before any warm-up. Report medians with spread, never personal bests; convert to centimetres and degrees so data survive setting changes; and after five to eight sessions compute each player's session-to-session noise per metric, declaring improvement only when a smoothed estimate moves well beyond it and stays there. Check transfer as a ladder: the trained loop; held-out variants (new backgrounds, footstep surfaces, HRTF sets and weapon profiles never used in training); a standardised in-game test such as the firing range or a bot match; and match statistics over many matches. With a handful of users, use within-person designs such as staggered starts or training one stage while holding another as an untrained control. Which in-game statistics Call of Duty exposes, and their reliability, were not researched.

The LLM stays confined to wording. Deterministic feature extraction and the statistical layer produce a compact profile of stage timings with uncertainty, flagged deviations and trial counts; the model turns that into a session note, selects drills from a closed catalogue by structured output, and any number absent from the profile is rejected. Grounding a model in aggregated summaries instead of raw streams follows health-coaching practice ([PH-LLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.06474v1)). Keep a template fallback and compare the two on your own group.

## Every game value lives in a data file with a verification status

### Four profile blocks

The spike's profile already carries a status string beside each value, and that pattern should extend to every field. The game block holds yaw, sensitivity, FOV slider and convention, ADS FOV mode (Affected or Independent), ADS sensitivity type (Legacy or Relative), monitor-distance coefficient, low-zoom and high-zoom multipliers, transition timing, weapon FOV, camera-movement scale and health per mode. The weapon block holds class, fire rate, ADS-in and ADS-out time, sprint-to-fire, hipfire spread and the ADS progress at which it collapses, hip and ADS movement speeds, idle sway, shots to kill, and recoil as three separable channels: view kick that moves the aim point and must be countered with the mouse (a per-shot yaw and pitch list plus a random cone and a recentering speed), gun kick that moves only the model and sight, and cosmetic shake. The optic block stores ADS field of view as an explicit angle for Independent and a zoom factor for Affected, the zoom tier, the FOV easing curve, a housing occlusion mask and a reticle; nominal "2x" labels are not true magnifications and should not be the stored value. The clutter block holds model silhouette scale at hip and ADS, muzzle flash size, brightness and duration, smoke emission, opacity and lifetime, tracer visibility and shake per shot, offered as preset levels (for example a heavy level resembling Modern Warfare II and a light level resembling Black Ops 6) because the developers changed these patch by patch. Audio parameters sit in a fifth, title-independent file: HRTF set, footstep library, level ranges, maskers, occlusion and reverb presets. Weapon, optic and clutter art is generic geometry; no game assets are needed or should be copied.

| Value | Status | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Projection and conversion math (cm/360 formula, horizontal-to-vertical FOV, size scaling) | Established | Geometry |
| Units of handling stats (ms, degrees, m/s, percent modifiers) | Established from official-note snippets | Patch notes, not read in full |
| Yaw 0.0066 | Community-derived, consistent | Three converter sites, two worked examples |
| FOV slider horizontal at 16:9 | Community-derived, arithmetic check passes | One published table |
| Fixed vertical FOV on other aspect ratios | Weak | One anecdote |
| Relative ADS formula and vertical-FOV basis | Community-derived | Forum summaries, calculator page |
| Coefficient default 1.33 | Community-derived | Forum and Steam posts |
| Legacy behaviour | Unknown, sources conflict | Must verify |
| ADS FOV per optic, both modes | Unverified | One guide value, one low-authority table |
| Transition timing behaviour and FOV easing curve | Unverified | Guide descriptions only |
| ADS, sprint-to-fire, time to kill, fire rate, speeds | Community-derived, single data site | CODMunity, Black Ops 6 and 7 only |
| Recoil pattern, randomness, recentering | No data | Must measure |
| Weapon and optic screen coverage, flash and smoke | No data | Must measure |
| Footstep level, attenuation, occlusion | No data, changes by season | Train across a range instead |

### The side-by-side verification checklist

Each preset stays labelled unverified until its test is run in the real game. First, hipfire cm/360 at a known DPI and sensitivity, which confirms yaw. Second, horizontal and vertical FOV at several slider values on 16:9 and on your own aspect ratio, which confirms the convention and the behaviour on other ratios. Third, ADS FOV per optic under Affected and Independent, measured from screenshots of an object of known size at known distance. Fourth, ADS cm/360 per optic under Legacy and under Relative at coefficients 0, 1.33 and 1.78, which confirms the formula and its vertical basis. Fifth, sensitivity during the transition for Instant, Gradual and After Zoom, using a constant scripted mouse motion and frame capture. Sixth, ADS time, sprint-to-fire, the FOV easing curve and the point at which hipfire spread ends, from high-frame-rate capture. Seventh, recoil pattern, randomness and recentering from the firing-range recoil wall, and whether bullets follow the sight under gun kick. Eighth, weapon and optic screen coverage at each weapon FOV setting, and flash and smoke opacity over a sustained burst. The first two can be done today with the existing spike; the rest need the ADS and weapon systems to exist first, and all eight must be repeated per title.

## Godot consequences and the path from the current spike

### What the audio and input findings mean for the engine

Godot's built-in 3D audio is panning and attenuation without HRTF, so a valid orienting drill needs a spatialiser. The best-known add-on, godot-steam-audio, targets Godot 4.4, describes itself as alpha, does not mention custom HRTF loading and was **archived read-only on 7 October 2026** ([GitHub](https://github.com/stechyo/godot-steam-audio)). An asset-store package called Nexus Resonance lists HRTF spatialisation and requires Godot 4.6 or later; it was seen only as a store listing and not evaluated ([Godot asset store](https://store.godotengine.org/asset/movec/nexus-resonance/)). Steam Audio itself supports custom HRTFs from SOFA files, with support varying by integration ([Steam Audio guide](https://valvesoftware.github.io/steam-audio/doc/fmod/guide.html)). Compatibility of any of these with Godot 4.7.2 is unverified. The options to evaluate, as engineering judgement, are a fork of the archived plugin or the store package after confirming real binaural rendering; a small GDExtension around Steam Audio's binaural effect feeding a generator stream, which also gives control of HRTF loading; or pre-rendering footstep clips against an HRTF at a dense grid of directions. Pre-rendering gives the simplest timing but handles fast camera turns poorly, and the update during the turn is what resolves front from back. The acceptance test for any option is concrete: true per-ear convolution, several loadable HRTF sets (the hedge against renderer-specific learning), smooth update during a fast mouse turn, and measured latency.

Audio onset time can only be estimated in Godot. Playback begins at the next mix chunk, and the documented estimate of heard time is the system time at the play call plus the time to next mix plus the output latency ([Godot docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/audio/sync_with_audio.html)); an engine issue, seen as a snippet and of unknown fix status, reports that the output-latency figure is inaccurate ([godotengine issue 38215](https://github.com/godotengine/godot/issues/38215)). Reaction time to a sound therefore needs a one-off loopback calibration per output device: play a click with a simultaneous screen flash, record the headphone output with a microphone or line loopback, and measure offset and jitter. Jitter of up to one mix chunk is the floor on per-trial precision unless audio is scheduled sample-accurately. Wired headphones should be recommended and the device name recorded per session. Windows spatial sound, headset virtual surround and Loudness Equalization would process the trainer's binaural output a second time, so the HRTF drills ask for plain stereo and record what is switched on, while advising players to otherwise train on the headphones they play with. Players raise volume to hear faint footsteps, so output level is fixed or capped, detection improves through practice at a set level, and sudden loud maskers are avoided; no safe-listening figure was sourced, and a primary reference is needed before stating any decibel limit.

On input, the spike records script-arrival times, which means every mouse report within a frame carries nearly the same timestamp and stage timings resolve no finer than a frame. The spike already counts events per frame; it must still quantify that batching at your mouse's polling rate, decide whether frame-level resolution is acceptable given stage durations of 200 to 300 ms, and, if it is not, obtain per-report timestamps through a native extension. End-to-end latency is unmeasured and the cm/360 comparison against the real game has not been done. Godot before 4.7.2 collapsed with 8 kHz mice in captured mode, which the installed version fixes ([Godot blog](https://godotengine.org/article/fixing-high-polling-rate-mice-on-windows/)). The ADS system adds a new requirement: sensitivity and FOV become functions of ADS progress, applied per mouse report under the selected transition option, with an automated test alongside the existing rotation tests.

### Phases from where the project stands

Phase 0 is partly done: raw counts drive rotation with yaw 0.0066, the profile is JSON, events log to CSV and the headless math tests pass. To close it, run checklist items one and two against the real game, measure end-to-end latency with your own mouse and display, quantify input batching and timestamp resolution, and run an audio spike that evaluates the spatialiser options against the acceptance test and performs the loopback calibration. Begin logging in-game baseline statistics now, since a baseline window costs nothing and cannot be collected retroactively.

Phase 1 builds the smallest complete loop with visual cues only: one target at a time at an unknown position anywhere around the player, a humanoid figure in simple clutter, ADS with timed FOV and sensitivity transition, hold-to-fire with a placeholder recoil pattern and shots-to-kill, a generic weapon and optic, and the full stage telemetry with the cold-probe session structure. Phase 2 runs checklist items three to eight for one weapon and optic in the chosen title and replaces placeholders with verified values, then adds the weapon model, optic housing, flash and smoke with the clutter on-off comparison. Phase 3 adds sound: the chosen spatialiser, footstep cues with maskers, no-cue and target-absent trials, the localisation drill with feedback, non-responder detection and volume capping. Phase 4 adds the measurement layer: noise model, stage decomposition against baseline and weapon floor, staircases, the isolated drills and the drill-selection bandit. Phase 5 adds the detection content (one biome, graded contrast, held-out scenes) and the dual-task condition in which the next cue arrives during a fight. Phase 6 adds the sensitivity-range procedure extended to ADS, the LLM note with its template fallback, the first within-person transfer check, and friends' machines with per-machine latency and audio calibration.

### Decisions still open for you

Which title to match is the first decision, and it is sharper than before: Black Ops 7 has the only weapon data retrieved, Modern Warfare 4 released on PC on 23 October 2026 by an official blog snippet and has none, and Warzone differs through armour and health. Matching one title first and treating the others as later profiles keeps the verification burden at eight tests. Decide which weapon and optic to verify first, and whether your group plays with Affected or Independent ADS FOV, since the two differ by roughly a factor of two in magnification on the derivation above. Decide whether friends see each other's data at all, or only cooperative group goals. Decide how much transfer-checking burden the group will accept, because baseline windows and withheld blocks cost enjoyment and without them the trainer cannot tell anyone whether it works. Decide whether the LLM runs locally or through a cloud service fed only the compact profile. Decide art scope: procedurally parameterised clutter and generic weapon geometry are the scope-safe default, and map likeness is the main route to scope creep. Two new decisions come with sound: whether to build or adopt the spatialiser, which the Phase 0 audio spike should inform, and whether the group will standardise on wired headphones and a declared Windows audio configuration so that reaction times are comparable across machines.

## Conclusion

Extending the trainer to sound and ADS moves it further from the evidence, and that changes what its measurements are worth. The earlier design could lean on a literature for pointing, practice schedules and camouflage; the stages now at the centre, turning toward a footstep with a mouse, aiming through a gain change, holding a burst through recoil and smoke, have no published human-performance data at all. The stage timings this trainer logs would be the first of their kind for its players, which makes the split-half reliability check and the clutter on-off and cue against no-cue comparisons the most informative things the project can produce early, ahead of any coaching.

The loop also changes what "weak aim" means. A player who loses fights can now be losing them in hearing, in turn direction, in search, in the ADS press or in the burst, and for several of these the mouse is not the limiting factor. The verification checklist is the gate on all of it: until yaw, ADS field of view, transition timing and recoil are confirmed against the live game, the loop trains a plausible shooter and not Call of Duty, and the specificity evidence says that difference is where transfer is lost.
